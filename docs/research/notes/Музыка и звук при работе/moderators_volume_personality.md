# Moderators of background music / sound effects on focused work: volume, tempo, task type, personality, WMC, ADHD, habituation, preference, Asian research, breaks

Context: focus-timer app, calm instrumental ambient soundscapes (Tabletop Audio), 25–90 min blocks, volume slider + on/off toggle.
Evidence-strength legend used below: [STRONG] peer-reviewed, controlled, reasonable N; [MODERATE] peer-reviewed but small N / single study / mixed replication; [WEAK] secondary summary, conference paper, thesis, press release, or details not verified in primary text.

---

## 1. Volume (dB) and tempo

### Takeaway
The best-documented harm comes from music that is both fast (~150 bpm) and loud (~72 dB): in Thompson et al. (2012) it significantly reduced reading comprehension, while slow, or fast-but-soft (~60 dB), music did not differ from silence. Moderate ambient noise (~70 dB) may help creative/abstract thinking, but high levels (~85 dB) hurt it. A sensible default is quiet (around 50–60 dB at the ear), slow-to-moderate tempo, with low arousal.

### Cited Findings
- **Thompson, Schellenberg & Letnic (2012), *Psychology of Music* 40(6):700–708, Australia (Macquarie Univ.).** [MODERATE: N=25, repeated measures] Music: Mozart Sonata for Two Pianos K.448, instrumental, manipulated in ProTools. Four conditions: slow/soft (110 bpm, 60 dB), slow/loud (110 bpm, 72.4 dB), fast/soft (150 bpm, 60 dB), fast/loud (150 bpm, 72.4 dB). Levels were measured at the headphones. Task: GMAT passages of about 500 words, 4 min reading plus 6 MCQs. — [Thompson et al. 2012 PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)
  - Results (mean correct out of 6): silent baseline 2.77 (SD 0.85, separate pilot sample N=16); slow/soft 2.38; slow/loud 2.43; fast/soft 2.96; **fast/loud 1.95**. Fast/loud was significantly below baseline (t(39)=2.37, p<.05), and the others did not differ from baseline (ps>.2). Tempo × intensity interaction F(1,23)=8.14, p<.01. Loudness had no effect at slow tempo (F<1); at fast tempo, loud was worse (F(1,23)=7.93, p<.01). — [Thompson et al. 2012 PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)
  - A pilot calibration found that a tempo change from 110 to 150 bpm was psychologically equivalent to a loudness change from 60 to 72.4 dB. — [same](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)
  - Musically trained participants outperformed untrained ones overall (F(1,23)=18.45), but training did not interact with the music conditions. — [same](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)
  - Caveats: the baseline came from a different (pilot) sample, the music was classical piano rather than ambient, and each listening episode lasted only 4 minutes.
  - The paper summary: [Bond Univ. record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/fast-and-loud-background-music-disrupts-reading-comprehension/)
- **Mehta, Zhu & Cheema (2012), *Journal of Consumer Research* 39(4):784, USA.** [MODERATE: 5 experiments; full N and effect sizes not verified] Found an inverted U. Moderate ambient noise (**70 dB**) improved creative-task performance compared with low noise (**50 dB**), while high noise (**85 dB**) reduced creativity. The proposed mechanism is that moderate disfluency induces abstract construal. — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/39/4/784/1798283); [Univ. of Illinois news](https://news.illinois.edu/research-too-much-too-little-noise-turns-off-consumers-creativity/)
  - The stimulus was ambient noise (café/road/construction mix), not music. The result concerns *creative* tasks only and should not be generalized to analytic or verbal tasks.
- **Gonzalez & Aiello (2019), *J. Experimental Psychology: Applied*, USA.** [MODERATE: N=142 undergraduates, 75% women] Varied music complexity and volume. Music generally **impaired complex-task** performance, and complex music **facilitated simple-task** performance. On the complex task, music complexity and volume made no difference; personality moderated the effect (see §3). — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2019-gonzalez.pdf); [WEF summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2019/03/should-you-listen-to-music-while-doing-intellectual-work-it-depends-on-the-music-the-task-and-your-personality/)
- **Su, He & Li (2023), *Frontiers in Psychology*, China (Fujian Normal Univ.).** [MODERATE] Chinese English majors read English texts with fast- vs slow-tempo music in an eye-tracking study. Fast tempo produced faster reading (significant main effect). Tempo mattered more for easy than for difficult texts. Slow-tempo music was "detrimental" for low-music-preference participants on *difficult* texts, while fast tempo benefited high-preference participants. Comprehension accuracy and N were not verified in my source. — [PMC10294666](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10294666/); [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1140959/pdf)
- **Korean middle-school study (2013), *Journal of Art Psychotherapy Research*.** [WEAK: abstract only, paywalled] N=104. Background music with **positive emotion and low arousal** helped attention. — [earticle.net](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A209535)

### Inferences
- For the app, "fast + loud" is the main danger zone. Ambient soundscapes with no strong beat (effectively slow or no tempo) at low level are the safest category in this literature.
- Default volume: start low. Thompson's "soft" level was 60 dB at the headphone, which was harmless at both tempos. Mehta's 50 dB vs 70 dB comparison suggests ~60–70 dB ambient noise is tolerable or even helpful for ideation, but 85 dB is harmful. A default of roughly 30–40% slider with a soft cap or warning at high levels seems reasonable. This is an inference, because device output mapped to dB varies.
- Su et al. (2023) and Thompson et al. (2012) together suggest that tempo effects depend on text difficulty and on the listener. Faster tempo may speed easy reading but is risky for hard material.

### Gaps
- I found no study testing **ambient/drone soundscapes** (no melody, no beat) at specific dB levels during 25–90-minute work blocks. All tempo studies used melodic music.
- The only verified dB anchors for music come from Thompson (60 vs 72.4 dB, headphone-measured). Other dB data (Mehta) concern noise, not music.
- I could not verify a dose-response curve between loudness and performance for music below 60 dB.

## 2. Task type: complex verbal vs routine/repetitive vs math/coding

### Takeaway
Music most reliably hurts **complex verbal/memory tasks** (reading comprehension, serial recall, prose recall), and lyrics make it worse. It tends to help or be neutral for **simple, repetitive, low-arousal tasks**, where it counters boredom (Fox & Embrey 1972; Gonzalez & Aiello 2019). Mental arithmetic shows weaker, less consistent harm. Field data from programmers (Lesiuk 2005) suggest mood and quality benefits with self-chosen music, but the evidence is weak.

### Cited Findings
- **Kämpfe, Sedlmeier & Renkewitz (2011) meta-analysis, *Psychology of Music*.** [STRONG as a meta-analysis, but limited by few computable effect sizes] The global effect of background music on adults was **null**, which the authors explain as specific effects averaging out. Music vs no music showed **small detrimental effects on memory**. The authors note that few studies allowed effect-size calculation. — [Gwern PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2011-kampfe.pdf)
- **Furnham & Strbac (2002), *Ergonomics* 45(3):203–217, UK.** [MODERATE: N=76, 38 introverts and 38 extraverts] Tasks were reading comprehension, prose recall, and mental arithmetic, done in silence, with UK garage music, or with office noise. Performance was overall worse with music or noise than in silence. The paper title is "Music is as distracting as noise." — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2002-furnham.pdf)
- **Perham & Vizard (2010/2011), *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, UK.** [MODERATE] Serial recall was worse with both liked and disliked music, and with changing-state speech, than in quiet or steady-state speech. This is the "irrelevant sound effect": acoustically *changing* sound disrupts order memory. — [Cardiff Met record](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/can-preference-for-background-music-mediate-the-irrelevant-sound-/)
- **Shih, Huang & Chiang (2012), *Work* 42, Taiwan.** [MODERATE: N=102] Background music **with lyrics** significantly reduced concentration and attention compared with instrumental music. — [Huang & Shih search record / IOS Press](https://content.iospress.com/articles/work/wor01410)
- **Fox & Embrey (1972), *Applied Ergonomics* 3(4):202–205, UK (Univ. of Birmingham).** [MODERATE/historical: data not verified] A series of experiments on **paced repetitive work** (manual assembly type) found that background music improved efficiency, even against machine noise. The rationale is arousal: repetitive tasks cause arousal to drop off. — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/1972-fox.pdf)
- **Gonzalez & Aiello (2019).** [MODERATE: N=142] Music **impaired complex** tasks and **complex music helped simple** tasks. — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2019-gonzalez.pdf)
- **Lesiuk (2005), *Psychology of Music* 33(2):173–191, Canada.** [WEAK–MODERATE: N=56 software developers at 4 companies, 5-week field study] Design was baseline week, then music allowed, then music removed in week 4, then music back. State positive affect and work quality were lowest, and time-on-task longest, in the no-music week. Later reviewers note the affect–music link was **not statistically significant**, and support relies largely on narrative responses. Developers chose their own music. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735605050650); [Graziotin et al. review, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.05330)
- **Kiss & Linnell (2021), *Psychological Research* 85(6):2313–2325, UK (Goldsmiths).** [MODERATE: N=40] On a low-demand sustained-attention task (PVT variant), **self-chosen preferred music** increased task focus by **reducing mind-wandering**. It did not affect external distraction, and it did **not** significantly improve RT or RT variability vs silence. A 2024 *Scientific Reports* follow-up with online, at-home samples replicated the task-focus increase. — [PMC8357712](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8357712/); [Sci Rep 2024](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/36045/8/s41598-024-60218-z.pdf)
- **HKU field pilot.** [WEAK] Self-selected music could aid learning, with the benefit clearer for **less demanding tasks**. — [HKU Hub](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/304830)

### Inferences
- The app could offer a "task type" hint. For reading, writing, memorizing, or learning new material, recommend very low volume or silence. For routine work (email triage, data entry, debugging repetitive steps), music on is reasonable and may reduce mind-wandering.
- Coding falls in between. It is verbal and working-memory-heavy (like reading) but often long and partly routine. The only direct coding evidence (Lesiuk) is a weak field study with self-chosen music, so silence-or-ambient remains the defensible default for hard coding.
- Tabletop Audio-style ambient tracks contain varying events (footsteps, voices, chimes). Under the irrelevant-sound/changing-state account, **discrete, changing events and any speech-like sounds** are the most disruptive for verbal and serial tasks. Steady, continuous textures are safer. The app should prefer steady loops for focus mode and avoid tracks with crowd chatter or intelligible voices.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of background music specifically during programming. Lesiuk is a field study of self-reported mood and quality.
- I found no verified effect sizes for math or arithmetic. Furnham & Strbac reported only a trend on that task.
- I did not verify Kämpfe et al.'s task-specific effect sizes (reading, memory) from the full text.

## 3. Personality (introversion/extraversion, boredom proneness, sensitivity to distraction)

### Takeaway
Classic UK studies show introverts are hurt more than extraverts by background music and noise on complex tasks, consistent with Eysenck's arousal theory. But replications are mixed, especially in Asian samples (Singapore, China), where the introvert–extravert gap mostly disappeared. Boredom proneness and the ability to ignore stimuli are newer, arguably more practical moderators.

### Cited Findings
- **Furnham & Bradley (1997), *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, UK.** [WEAK–MODERATE: N=20, 10 introverts and 10 extraverts] Pop music vs silence. Music impaired immediate recall for both groups. On delayed recall, introverts with music scored lower than extraverts with music and lower than introverts in silence. Reviews differ on which interactions were significant, and the sample was very small. — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/1997-furnham.pdf)
- **Furnham & Strbac (2002), *Ergonomics*, UK.** [MODERATE: N=76] The predicted personality × sound interaction was significant **only for reading comprehension**, with a trend on prose recall and arithmetic. Introverts did worse with music or office noise than in silence; extraverts showed no difference across conditions. — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2002-furnham.pdf)
- **Dobbs, Furnham & McClelland (2011), *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, UK.** [MODERATE] Tasks were complex cognitive tests (e.g., abstract reasoning, verbal, general ability) in silence, with UK garage music, or with office noise. Introverts' performance was harmed more by music and noise; in silence, introverts and extraverts performed equally. The ordering was **silence > music > noise**, so noise was more disruptive than music. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/noise-and-music-are-more-distracting-introverts-work); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1568420/)
- **Koo, McClelland & Furnham (2017?), China.** [WEAK–MODERATE: thesis/BI Open record] N=92 Chinese participants (59 female) did three cognitive tasks with Chinese pop songs, office noise, or silence. This was a **partial** replication. A UCL thesis notes a failure to replicate distraction effects in Chinese participants and attributes it to **habituation to noisy environments**. — [BI Open](https://biopen.bi.no/bi-xmlui/handle/11250/2500086); [UCL PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1568420/1/McClelland_A3.pdf)
- **McClelland, Lim, Furnham & McClelland (2020), UK vs Singapore.** [MODERATE] Found **no** performance differences between sound conditions, and no introvert–extravert differences across conditions, in either sample, with three exceptions. The authors conclude the effect held for Western but not Asian samples. — [UCL PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10132491/1/McClelland_Lim%2C%20Furnham%20%26%20McClelland%2020201.pdf)
- **Gonzalez & Aiello (2019).** [MODERATE: N=142] **Boredom proneness / preference for external stimulation** moderated effects. On a simple task, people low in boredom proneness did best with complex music, while boredom-prone people did best with no music or simple music. On the complex task, low-boredom-prone people benefited from any music, while boredom-prone people did better with **no music**. Press framed the findings as preliminary. — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2019-gonzalez.pdf); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/mind-brain/should-you-listen-to-music-while-doing-intellectual-work-it-depends-on-the-music-the-task-and-your-personality-2631245619)
  - Note: this partly *contradicts* the naive intuition that stimulation-seekers want music. The authors argue that for boredom-prone people, the complex task itself gives enough stimulation.
- **Ohio State "music misfit" study (2025, *Journal of Applied Psychology*).** [WEAK: known only via press/Japanese summary] Music that doesn't fit the listener lowered energy and focus. The effect was strongest in people who **struggle to ignore background stimuli**. — [Nazology (JP)](https://nazology.kusuguru.co.jp/archives/174787); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/music-productivity-psychology-28562/)

### Inferences
- An onboarding question such as "Do you find background sound distracting?" or "Are you easily distracted by noise?" is probably more predictive and actionable than an introversion questionnaire. Introversion effects are small-sample and not robust cross-culturally.
- Introverts and people sensitive to distraction should get a lower default volume, and a gentle suggestion to try silence for reading-heavy blocks.
- Across Dobbs et al. (2011), office noise was more disruptive than music. This supports offering soundscapes as a *masker* in noisy, open-plan environments rather than as an addition in already-quiet rooms (inference).

### Gaps
- The exact Dobbs et al. (2011) sample size and effect sizes were not verified in the primary text.
- No study found tested personality × *ambient soundscape* (as opposed to pop or garage music).

## 4. Working-memory capacity (WMC)

### Takeaway
Limited evidence suggests that higher WMC buffers the harm from music on reading comprehension. Lower-WMC listeners are more vulnerable. The evidence base is thin (theses, single studies).

### Cited Findings
- **UTC master's thesis (2015, Univ. of Tennessee at Chattanooga; chair Jill Shelton).** [WEAK: thesis] Undergraduates did reading comprehension and math with music or in silence. Music caused a significant overall decline, and **WMC moderated the effect on reading comprehension** (not reported for math). Sometimes cited as Christopher & Shelton (2017); I could not confirm the published version. — [UTC Scholar](https://scholar.utc.edu/theses/171)
- **Frontiers in Psychology (31 Oct 2017), "influence of background music on learning … role of working memory capacity," DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01902.** [Unverified authorship and results] — [Frontiers subject page](https://www.frontiersin.org/subjects/learning-with-background-music)
- **"Working memory modulates the effect of music on word learning," Univ. of Reading.** [MODERATE, details not extracted] — [CentAUR PDF](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/109465/1/working-memory-modulates-the-effect-of-music-on-word-learning.pdf)
- A secondary source cites **de la Mora Velasco & Hirumi (2020) meta-analysis**: classical music was associated with the best memory-test performance and pop with the least positive effects. [WEAK: not verified directly] — cited in [musicscience.net PDF](https://musicscience.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/richardson.pdf)

### Inferences
- Users who report being easily overloaded, and tasks with heavy WM load (learning new material, reading dense text), should default to silence or very low volume.

### Gaps
- No verified effect sizes for the WMC × music interaction. I could not confirm the Christopher & Shelton (2017) publication details.

## 5. ADHD / inattention

### Takeaway
There is moderate evidence that **white/steady noise** helps inattentive children and children with ADHD on memory and verbal tasks, while it can *hurt* highly attentive children. This is the "moderate brain arousal" idea. For **music**, the ADHD evidence is weaker: mostly neutral, with a minority benefiting. Video is clearly distracting.

### Cited Findings
- **Söderlund and colleagues (white noise, children with ADHD).** [MODERATE: small samples] White noise improved memory and verbal task performance in children with ADHD, and improved inattention symptoms but not hyperactivity symptoms. In high-attention children, white noise **worsened** performance. — [CHADD](https://chadd.org/adhd-weekly/could-white-noise-help-you-stay-on-task/); [Huntington Helps summary](https://secureapplication.huntingtonhelps.com/resources/adhd-blog/effects-of-media-on-adhd-kids)
- **2022 pilot, *IJERPH* 19(12):7283.** [WEAK–MODERATE: pilot] Reported verbal working-memory gains with noise in children with ADHD. — [doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19127283](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19127283)
- **Univ. of Southampton (Helps et al.), school pupils without diagnosis.** [MODERATE] Background noise **improved** performance for inattentive children and **worsened** it for attentive children. — [Southampton ePrints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/375557)
- **Pelham et al. (2011), boys with ADHD, music vs video.** [WEAK: via secondary summaries] Across three studies, background **music had no effect** on most ADHD children's performance (about 12% did more work with music). **Video** produced significant distraction, especially for ADHD boys. — [GoodTherapy](https://www.goodtherapy.org/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/11040); [Huntington Helps](https://secureapplication.huntingtonhelps.com/resources/adhd-blog/effects-of-media-on-adhd-kids)

### Inferences
- For an "ADHD / easily bored" profile, a steady, broadband soundscape (rain, wind, room tone) is better supported than melodic music. It may help more than silence for some users but should remain optional.
- The same noise that helps inattentive users can impair highly attentive users. This is a strong argument for **user control and per-user defaults** rather than "music on for everyone."

### Gaps
- Almost all ADHD evidence is in **children**. I found no verified controlled study in **adults with ADHD** doing knowledge work with ambient soundscapes.
- The dB levels used in the white-noise studies were not verified in this search, so I am not reporting them.

## 6. Habituation over time, preference, and control

### Takeaway
There is little direct evidence that music's distraction fades within minutes. An older Japanese study found that the effects of background sound were **stable** across repeated sessions, even as overall performance improved through practice. Preference matters in both directions. Strongly liked *and* strongly disliked music both affect attention (Huang & Shih 2011), and liked music can even be *more* disruptive for serial recall (Perham & Sykora 2012). Self-chosen music reduces mind-wandering on easy tasks (Kiss & Linnell). Control (the ability to switch off) is plausibly important but under-tested.

### Cited Findings
- **Japanese study, *Journal of the Acoustical Society of Japan (E)* 17(5):239 (1996).** [MODERATE: details from abstract] Found **no habituation** to background speech. Overall performance improved with repeated sessions, but "the effects of the experimental background sounds are stable." — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast1980/17/5/17_5_239/_article)
- **Office-noise habituation study, *Noise & Health* (2010).** [MODERATE: results not extracted] Explicitly asked whether, and how fast, habituation to office noise (including speech) develops. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/709b3dd76ad44382a2d2c8fffd7d979d)
- **2025 RCT, "Impact of background music on flow, work engagement and task performance" (*Behavioral Sciences* 15(4):416).** [MODERATE: details not extracted] Discusses habituation reducing music's benefit over repeated exposure and recommends **rotating selections**. — [PMC12024392](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12024392/); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328x/15/4/416)
- **Chinese participants and habituation (UCL thesis).** [WEAK] The null distraction effect in Chinese participants was interpreted as habituation to noisy everyday environments. This is long-term, culture-level habituation, not within-session. — [UCL PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1568420/1/McClelland_A3.pdf)
- **Huang & Shih (2011), *Work* 38:383–387, Taiwan.** [MODERATE: RCT, N=89 workers, age 19–28] Background music influenced attention, and this "has more to do with listener fondness for the music than with type." The stronger the like *or* dislike, the larger the effect on attention. The authors recommend **avoiding music that workers strongly like or dislike**. — [DOI 10.3233/WOR-2011-1141](https://www.doi.org/10.3233/WOR-2011-1141); [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2011-huang.pdf)
- **Perham & Sykora (2012), *Applied Cognitive Psychology*.** [MODERATE] Serial recall was significantly **worse with liked music than with disliked music**. — [Cardiff Met](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/disliked-music-can-be-better-for-performance-than-liked-music/); [Gwern PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2012-perham.pdf)
- **Perham & Vizard (2010/11).** Liked and disliked music impaired recall equally vs quiet. — [Cardiff Met](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/can-preference-for-background-music-mediate-the-irrelevant-sound-/)
- **Kiss & Linnell (2021; 2024).** [MODERATE: N=40 lab, plus online replications] **Self-selected preferred** music increased task focus by reducing mind-wandering on a low-demand vigilance task, without RT gains. — [PMC8357712](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8357712/)
- **Su, He & Li (2023), China.** [MODERATE] Music-listening preference (high vs low) moderated tempo effects. Low-preference people were harmed by slow music on difficult texts. — [PMC10294666](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10294666/)

### Inferences
- Neutral, moderately pleasant, non-salient soundscapes fit the "avoid strongly liked/disliked" guidance better than users' favourite songs. Tabletop Audio-style ambient is reasonably aligned with this, but very "eventful" tracks may attract attention.
- No good evidence says distraction reliably fades after N minutes, so the app should **not** promise "it gets easier after 5 minutes." Offering rotation across sessions may reduce boredom or habituation of any *benefit* (weak evidence).
- A prominent, one-tap mute is justified: preference is idiosyncratic, and the costs fall mainly on hard tasks.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring the minute-by-minute time course of music distraction within a 25–90-minute work block.
- I found no controlled study isolating *perceived control* (ability to switch off) as a moderator of music effects. The noise-control literature (Glass & Singer-type paradigms) was not searched here.

## 7. Asian research (China, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Singapore)

### Takeaway
Asian studies broadly match Western findings that lyrics hurt and that preference and arousal matter. Taiwan's Huang & Shih (2011) and Shih et al. (2012) are the most cited workplace studies. EEG work in China suggests music raises cognitive effort without changing behavioral accuracy. Notably, the Furnham-school introvert/extravert distraction effect largely **failed to replicate** in Chinese and Singaporean samples, possibly due to habituation to noisy environments.

### Cited Findings
- **Taiwan: Huang & Shih (2011), *Work*.** N=89. Preference intensity matters more than genre. — [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.3233/WOR-2011-1141)
- **Taiwan: Shih, Huang & Chiang (2012), *Work* 42.** N=102. Lyrics significantly worsened concentration vs instrumental music. — [IOS Press](https://content.iospress.com/articles/work/wor01410)
- **China: Du et al. (2020), EEG, Chinese postgraduates.** [MODERATE: N=39] Reading with high-arousal music, low-arousal music, or silence. Both music groups showed a significantly larger **N400** (interpreted as more cognitive effort). **Behavioral accuracy and completion time did not differ** between groups. — [search summary of HKU/ BVS records](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/gim/resource/en/wpr-828130)
- **China: Tang et al. (2017), ERP, BIO Web of Conferences.** [WEAK: conference paper] Flute and zither background music produced larger P300 amplitudes and shorter latencies vs quiet, interpreted as enhanced attention. — [BIO Web of Conferences](https://www.bio-conferences.org/10.1051/bioconf/20170803019)
- **China: Su, He & Li (2023), *Frontiers*.** Eye-tracking; tempo × difficulty × preference (see §1). — [PMC10294666](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10294666/)
- **China: Koo, McClelland & Furnham (2017).** N=92. Chinese pop, office noise, silence; partial replication of introvert/extravert effects. — [BI Open](https://biopen.bi.no/bi-xmlui/handle/11250/2500086)
- **Singapore: McClelland et al. (2020).** No sound-condition or personality effects in the Asian sample. — [UCL PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10132491/1/McClelland_Lim%2C%20Furnham%20%26%20McClelland%2020201.pdf)
- **Hong Kong: HKU field pilot.** Self-selected music aided learning more on less demanding tasks. [WEAK] — [HKU Hub](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/304830)

- **Korea: RISS record, university students.** Background music associated with better learning attitudes and well-being. Methods unknown. [WEAK] — [RISS](https://www.riss.kr/link?id=A345824)

### Inferences
- Cross-cultural differences in baseline noise exposure may weaken distraction effects. For Asian users in dense, noisy settings, soundscapes may function mainly as masking (speculative).
- EEG findings (larger N400/P300) point to "more effort or engagement, no performance difference." Music may make work feel different without making it better or worse on short tasks.

### Gaps
- I could not access CNKI full texts. Mainland Chinese-language studies are under-represented here.
- No Japanese or Korean study with verified sample sizes and effect sizes on BGM during office knowledge work was found.

## 8. Music during breaks vs during work

### Takeaway
Evidence for music as a *break* activity (mood recovery) is promising but modest. A 15-minute lunchtime music break raised later work engagement but not afternoon vigour or stress (Limerick 2022, N=36). Music before a task can lift mood, while music *during* demanding tasks competes for resources. This supports offering music in breaks even for users who choose silence during work.

### Cited Findings
- **University of Limerick (2022), randomized trial.** [MODERATE: N=36 workers] A 15-minute music-listening lunch break was followed by higher work engagement the next week. No significant differences in afternoon vigour or stress. — [RTÉ Brainstorm](https://rte.ie/brainstorm/2022/0920/1324350-employees-lunchtime-music-listening-afternoon-productivity-research); [Behav. Sci. 13:15](https://mdpi.com/2076-328x/13/1/15)
- **"Effects of music advertised to support focus on mood and processing speed," *PLOS One* (2025).** [MODERATE: N=196, online MTurk] Compared "work flow" and "deep focus" playlists vs pop hits vs office noise during a flanker task. Only "work flow" music produced significant, positively correlated improvements in mood and performance (faster responses over time, similar accuracy). — [PMC11819607](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11819607/)
- **Lifehacker Japan (Inc. reprint).** Cites a 2010 study where serial recall was worse with music than in silence, and notes that music lifts mood *before* a task but hinders demanding work. A neuroscientist suggests 15-minute music breaks every 2–3 hours. [WEAK: popular press] — [Lifehacker JP](https://www.lifehacker.jp/article/170122_20170122_neuroscience_BGM/)
- **Lesiuk (2005).** Narrative responses described music as improving mood and helping see design problems more clearly. [WEAK] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735605050650)

### Inferences
- A sensible app pattern: optional silence or very low ambient during focus blocks for hard verbal work, and an upbeat or pleasant track offered during the 5–15-minute break. This captures the mood benefit without the dual-task cost.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing the *same* music during breaks vs during work, measured on next-block performance.

## Cross-cutting summary for app defaults (inference, grounded in the above)
- **Default sound:** steady, low-arousal, instrumental ambient with no speech or lyrics and few salient events. Avoid fast rhythmic or "energetic" tracks during focus blocks (Thompson 2012; Shih 2012; Korean 2013).
- **Default volume:** low. Thompson's harmless "soft" level was 60 dB at the ear; harms appeared at ~72 dB combined with fast tempo. Mehta found 85 dB harmful even for creativity. Consider a cap or warning at high slider positions.
- **When silence is better:** reading or learning dense material, writing, memorization, hard coding or debugging, and exam prep (Furnham & Strbac 2002; Kämpfe 2011; Perham studies; WMC thesis).
- **When sound may help:** repetitive or boring tasks, low-demand vigilance, and noisy environments as a masker (Fox & Embrey 1972; Gonzalez & Aiello 2019; Kiss & Linnell 2021; Dobbs 2011 noise > music).
- **Who should turn it down or off:** self-reported noise-sensitive or easily distracted people, introverts on reading tasks (weak, not robust in Asian samples), boredom-prone people on complex tasks (Gonzalez & Aiello), and low-WMC users on reading.
- **Who might benefit from steady noise:** inattentive or ADHD profiles (child data; adult data lacking).
- **Preference:** avoid tracks users strongly love or strongly dislike for deep work (Huang & Shih 2011; Perham & Sykora 2012). Rotation is weakly supported.
- **Breaks:** offering music in breaks for mood is low-risk (Limerick 2022).
