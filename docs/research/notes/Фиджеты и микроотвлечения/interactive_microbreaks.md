# Interactive micro-diversions during focused work: restorative micro-breaks or distraction?

Context: a focus-timer app shows interactive animated smoke around the timer that users can click and drag. Options under consideration: (A) interactive during work blocks, (B) interactive only during breaks, (C) non-interactive or slower during focus.

Evidence-strength legend used below: **[RCT/lab]** randomized lab experiment; **[diary]** experience-sampling or daily-diary field study (within-person, correlational); **[survey]** cross-sectional; **[MA]** meta-analysis or systematic review; **[guideline]** standard or design principle, not empirical; **[pop]** popular or industry claim with weak or no peer-reviewed support.

---

## 1. Brief diversions and vigilance: Ariga & Lleras (2011), replications, planned vs unplanned diversions

### Takeaway
Ariga & Lleras' "brief goal switches prevent the vigilance decrement" result has not held up well. A large conceptual replication by the same paradigm family (N = 498, New Zealand) found **no benefit** of brief task switches. A follow-up (N = 266 + 521) found that **complete rest** restored vigilance, while switching to another engaging task gave little or no benefit. The planned-vs-unplanned literature suggests that **self-initiated** diversions (the user decides to go and play) cost more than **scheduled or externally cued** ones. The decision to break away is itself costly.

### Cited Findings
- **Ariga & Lleras 2011, *Cognition* 118(3):439–443 (USA, University of Illinois) [RCT/lab].** Participants did a roughly 50-minute visual vigilance task while holding digits in memory. Performance dropped sharply when the digits were recalled only at the end. When participants were sporadically asked to recall the digits during the task (two brief "switches"), the vigilance decrement was averted. The authors read this as **goal habituation**, not resource depletion, and proposed that "deactivating and reactivating your goals allows you to stay focused." — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/); [ScienceDaily press release](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110208131529.htm)
- Only the switch group saw the digits during the 50-minute task (twice), so the manipulation was **planned, experimenter-imposed and very brief**. It was not a self-chosen play break. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110208131529.htm)
- **Failed conceptual replication: Helton & Russell 2012, *Experimental Brain Research* 219:37–46, "Brief mental breaks and content-free cues may not keep you focused" (New Zealand, University of Canterbury; N = 498) [RCT/lab].** All experimental groups (brief task switches, content-free cues, control) showed an **equivalent** vigilance decrement in perceptual sensitivity and response latency. Self-reported workload and task-unrelated thoughts did not differ. The study found **no support for the goal-habituation account**. — [SpringerMedicine abstract page](https://www.springermedicine.com/brief-mental-breaks-and-content-free-cues-may-not-keep-you-focus/25692108)
- **Helton & Russell 2015, *Cognition* 134:165–173, "Rest is best" (New Zealand) [RCT/lab].**
  - **Design.** Experiment 1 had N = 266 students and Experiment 2 had N = 521. A visuospatial vigilance task was interrupted for about 1.76 minutes by one of: complete rest, a different (letter) vigilance task, verbal working-memory, spatial working-memory, or continuing the primary task.
  - **Experiment 2 sensitivity (A′, pre → post interruption).** Rest went from .860 to .913 (t = 2.29, p = .024, n = 130). Verbal memory went from .859 to .882 (p = .063, n = 96). Letter detection went from .867 to .871 (p = .71, n = 95). Spatial memory went from .863 to .853 (p = .38, n = 91). Continuous went from .874 to .831 (t = 3.77, p < .001, n = 109).
  - **Experiment 1 response time.** The post-interruption increase was 18.1 ms after rest, versus +32.9 ms more for the letter-task switch and 70.1 ms for continuous work.
  - **Authors' conclusion.** The decrement reflects "repeated use of particular executive resources," plus "domain specific interference when the primary task and activities during a break make use of the same resources."
  - **Source.** [Helton & Russell 2015 PDF (interruptions.net mirror)](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf)
- A broader review of vigilance-enhancement techniques lists rest breaks, task switches and other countermeasures, and treats the mechanism as still debated (resource versus underload/mindlessness accounts). — [Al-Shargie et al., review, PMC6721323](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6721323/)
- **Planned or external vs self-initiated diversions: Katidioti, Borst, van Vugt & Taatgen 2016, *Computers in Human Behavior*, "Interrupt me: External interruptions are less disruptive than self-interruptions" (Netherlands, University of Groningen) [RCT/lab].**
  - Experiment 1 was behavioral (N = 28) and Experiment 2 used pupillometry (N = 21).
  - Self-interruptions slowed main-task completion more than external interruptions at comparable moments.
  - Resumption time did **not** differ between the two. The extra cost came from the **decision to self-interrupt (about 1 s)**, with pupil dilation rising before the switch.
  - Katidioti & Taatgen (2014) also report that people self-interrupt at suboptimal moments.
  - Sources: [Katidioti et al. 2016 PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Katidioti-ComputHumBehav16.pdf); [BPS Research Digest summary](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/interrupting-yourself-can-be-more-disruptive-being-interrupted-someone-else); [RUG project page](https://www.rug.nl/research/bernoulli/groups/cognitive-modeling/research/phd-project_-towards-safe-and-productive-multitasking)
- A software-engineering field study (arXiv) independently reports that external interruptions were less disruptive than self-interruptions in practice. — [Abad et al., arXiv 1805.05508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.05508)

### Inferences
- The popular takeaway "brief diversions boost focus" (widely spread from the 2011 press release) **overstates** the evidence. The best-powered follow-ups (N = 498; N = 787 total in "Rest is best") favor **genuine rest** over switching to another engaging activity. Switching to a task that uses the same modality as the main task (visual-spatial, for example) may even add interference.
- An interactive smoke toy is visual-spatial and motor. For most screen-based knowledge work (reading, coding, visual layout) it shares resources with the primary task. Under Helton & Russell's account, that makes it a **poor break activity during the work block** and a weaker restorer than looking away or resting.
- The Ariga & Lleras switches were **scheduled and externally cued**. A clickable smoke toy during a work block invites **self-interruptions**, which the Katidioti results tie to an extra decision cost and to badly timed switches. This argues against option A and for option B, where any play is scheduled and break-bound.

### Gaps
- Ariga & Lleras' exact sample size and effect sizes could not be verified from the sources fetched (often cited as about 84 participants in 4 groups; unverified here).
- No direct, preregistered replication of the Ariga & Lleras digit-switch procedure was found. A 2023 APS poster on vigilance replication tested a different question.
- Vigilance tasks are monotonous detection tasks. Generalizing them to creative or complex knowledge work is an extrapolation.

## 2. Short casual games and playful breaks vs rest vs phone/social media

### Takeaway
Casual games reliably improve **mood, energy and felt recovery** after strain, with small to moderate effects in small samples, some industry-funded. Evidence that a game break improves **subsequent cognitive performance** is weaker. The meta-analytic evidence for micro-breaks shows well-being gains (d ≈ 0.35) but a **non-significant** overall performance effect (d = 0.16), and no performance benefit after cognitively demanding tasks. Phone-based breaks recover least. In a daily-diary study, "cognitive" micro-break activities **worsened** the effect of work demands on negative affect, while relaxation and social breaks helped.

### Cited Findings
- **Albulescu et al. 2022, *PLOS ONE* 17(8): e0272460 (Romania) [MA].**
  - **Scope.** 22 independent samples from 19 records, N = 2,335. Micro-breaks were defined as breaks of 10 minutes or less.
  - **Well-being.** Vigor d = 0.36 (k = 9, n = 913, p < .001). Fatigue reduction d = 0.35 (k = 9, n = 803, p < .001).
  - **Performance.** The overall effect was d = 0.16 (k = 15, p = .116, not significant). It was significant **only for tasks with lower cognitive demand** (clerical or creative-routine).
  - **Break length.** Meta-regression showed that longer breaks gave a larger performance boost. The authors suggest that recovering from highly depleting tasks may need more than 10 minutes.
  - **Sources.** [DOAJ abstract](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/962663)
- **Kim, Park & Niu 2017, *Journal of Organizational Behavior* 38(1):28–44 (South Korea; 86 office workers × 10 workdays, 842 day-level observations) [diary].** Relaxation and social micro-break activities **buffered** the link between daily work demands and end-of-day negative affect. Nutrition intake (snacks, drinks) had no significant effect. **Cognitive micro-break activities aggravated** the effect of demands on negative affect. — [Search-verified abstract via MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/51909-micro-break-activities-at-work-to-recover-from-daily-work-demands)
- **Kang & Kurtzberg 2019, *Journal of Behavioral Addictions* 8(3):395–403 (USA, Rutgers; N = 414 undergraduates) [RCT/lab].**
  - **Design.** Participants solved anagrams with a mid-task break of the same content (choosing items for a shopping list) done on a **cell phone, a computer, or paper**, or took no break.
  - **Result.** Phone-break participants showed the poorest recovery. They took **19% longer** on the second half and solved **22% fewer** problems than the other break groups combined.
  - **Sources.** [PMC7044622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7044622); [Neuroscience News summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/mental-break-cell-phone-14742/)
- **Collins, Cox, Wilcock & Sethu-Jones 2019, *JMIR Mental Health* 6(7): e12853 (UK, UCL) [RCT/lab + field].**
  - **Lab study (N = 45 students, aged 19–36).** After a work task inducing need for recovery, participants played a casual puzzle game (Block! Hexa Puzzle), used a mindfulness app (Headspace), or used a **fidget spinner** (non-media control). The game group felt **more energized and less tired**. The mindfulness and fidget-spinner groups showed the opposite. There were **no differences** in Recovery Experience scores.
  - **Field study (N = 20 working professionals, 5 days).** The game group felt more relaxed by the end of the week than the mindfulness group.
  - **Sources.** [PMC6670275](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670275); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10078882/)
  - The fidget spinner is a toy-like, tactile "diversion" control. It did **not** restore energy in this sample, which bears directly on a draggable smoke toy. — [PMC6670275](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670275)
- **Russoniello, O'Brien & Parks 2009, *Journal of CyberTherapy & Rehabilitation* 2(1):53–66 (USA, East Carolina University) [RCT/lab].**
  - **Design.** Adults aged 19–57 played one of three PopCap casual games (Bejeweled 2, Bookworm Adventures, Peggle) or searched the web (control) for about 15–20 minutes. Outcomes were EEG, HRV and POMS mood.
  - **Results.** All games improved mood (less fatigue, tension, confusion and anger; more vigor). Only Bejeweled reduced physiological stress (HRV) versus control. The press release figures ("54% reduction", "573% mood improvement") come from a **press write-up, not the paper**.
  - **Funding conflict.** **PopCap funded the study** and supplied the games.
  - **Sources.** [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Carmen-Russoniello-2/publication/289131468_The_effectiveness_of_casual_video_games_in_improving_mood_and_decreasing_stress/links/589b371492851c942ddac68d/The-effectiveness-of-casual-video-games-in-improving-mood-and-decreasing-stress.pdf); [ECU news](https://news.ecu.edu/?p=15871); [FierceHealthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/healthcare/study-casual-video-games-demonstrate-ability-to-relieve-stress-improve-mood-potential)
- **Reinecke 2009a, *Cyberpsychology & Behavior* 12(4):461–465, "Games at Work" (Germany; N = 833 users of the GameDuell portal) [survey].**
  - 46.6% (383) reported playing games during working hours.
  - Recovery experience during play was associated with more frequent play at work, and work-related fatigue was associated with recovery through play.
  - Employees with **less social support** from colleagues and supervisors played at work more.
  - The design is **correlational, from a self-selected gamer sample**.
  - Sources: [Mainz bibliography](https://bibliographie.ub.uni-mainz.de/catalog/publication-82572); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/games-recover-stres/)
- **Reinecke 2009b, *Journal of Media Psychology* 21(3):126–142, "Games and Recovery" (Germany; N = 1,614, aged 12–56) [survey].** Gamers who reported stronger recovery experiences played more after stressful situations. — [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/games-recover-stres/); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/gamers-find-it-easier-relax-and-detach-work)
- **Reinecke, Klatt & Krämer 2011, *Media Psychology* 14(2):192–215 (Germany; N = 160) [RCT/lab].**
  - **Design.** After a strain-inducing work task, participants were randomized to play a video game, watch a recording of that game, watch an animated clip, or a no-media control.
  - **Results.** Interactive and non-interactive media gave different recovery patterns. Recovery experience related to enjoyment, energetic arousal and **objective cognitive performance**, and enjoyment mediated the effect on energetic arousal.
  - **Source.** [CAS library record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edb,61012079); [Reinecke 2008 Meaningful Play abstract](https://meaningfulplay.msu.edu/proceedings2008/abstract.php?paperid=33)
- **Rieger, Reinecke and colleagues, game demand and recovery.** A 2019 follow-up from the same research line (Wulf et al., *Media and Communication* 7(4)) examined how a game's cognitive demand shapes recovery. Its specific results were not verified here. — [Cogitatio open access](https://www.cogitatiopress.com/mediaandcommunication/article/view/2297)

### Inferences
- The "games restore" evidence mostly comes from **post-work or scheduled-break** settings (10–20 minutes of play after a strain task) and measures **mood and energy** rather than next-block performance. None of it tests a toy that is available **continuously during** a work block.
- Small effects plus null performance effects (Albulescu 2022) and the Kim et al. finding that cognitive micro-breaks worsen affect suggest that **the content of a break matters**. Low-demand, non-screen, relaxing or social activities beat engaging screen activities for the next work block.
- The Kang & Kurtzberg result (same break content, worse on a phone) is consistent with a "device-associated pull" or attention-residue explanation. A toy that lives **on the same screen as the work** may behave more like the phone condition than like stepping away. This is an inference; the study did not test on-screen toys.
- Collins et al.'s fidget-spinner control (no energizing effect) is the closest empirical analog to "fiddling with an interactive element." It suggests that low-challenge tactile play **is not restorative in itself**. The restorative ingredients the authors propose (relaxation, mastery, immersion, control) need a real game, not a toy.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares "on-screen toy available during work" with "no toy" for knowledge-work performance.
- Several game-recovery studies are small (N = 20–45) and student-based, and one is industry-funded (Russoniello). Effect sizes were often not reported in accessible abstracts.
- The exact definition and examples of the "cognitive" micro-break category in Kim et al. 2017 (for example reading, web browsing or email) could not be verified from the abstract.

## 3. Gamification and toy-like UI in productivity tools: motivation or distraction?

### Takeaway
Gamification produces small positive effects on average. They depend heavily on context and user, are vulnerable to **novelty effects**, and motivational and behavioral effects are less robust than cognitive ones. Streaks motivate while intact, but **breaking a streak demotivates** people and causes drop-off. Evidence on **fidget-like interaction during an attention-demanding task** points toward **impaired** encoding and more zoning out, not better focus.

### Cited Findings
- **Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa 2014, HICSS-47 (Finland) [MA/review].** In a review of the peer-reviewed empirical gamification studies (commonly cited as 24), gamification had **positive effects that depend heavily on context and on users**. Studies repeatedly flagged context, user characteristics and **novelty effects** as caveats. The review is a 2013 snapshot. — [Full text PDF](https://www.lescahiersdelinnovation.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/2014-hamari_et_al-does_gamification_work.pdf); [Aalto record](https://research.aalto.fi/fi/publications/does-gamification-work-a-literature-review-of-empirical-studies-o/)
- **Sailer & Homner 2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:77–112 (Germany) [MA].**
  - Cognitive outcomes: g = 0.49 (k = 19, N = 1,686).
  - Motivational outcomes: g = 0.36 (k = 16, N = 2,246).
  - Behavioral outcomes: g = 0.25 (k = 9, N = 951).
  - Only the cognitive effect was stable in high-rigor studies. Motivational and behavioral effects were less stable, and heterogeneity was high.
  - Sources: [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [Augsburg OPUS PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf)
- **Silverman & Barasch 2023, *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6):1095–1117 (USA) [RCT/lab + field, 7 studies].**
  - Logged streaks (a behavior repeated 3 or more times in a row) that are shown as **intact** increase later engagement compared with streaks shown as **broken**. This depends on how the log represents the behavior, not on the behavior itself.
  - The broken-streak penalty is larger when people blame themselves and smaller when the streak can be "repaired."
  - Barasch: people "will just drop off once their streaks are broken."
  - Sources: [UDSpace](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [CU Boulder summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)
- **Soares & Storm 2020, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 34(1):277–284 (USA, UC Santa Cruz) [RCT/lab].**
  - **Experiment 1 (N = 98 undergraduates).** Participants watched a 15-minute video lecture with or without a fidget spinner. Spinner users recalled significantly **less** and reported **more zoning out**.
  - **Experiment 2 (N = 48).** Participants who **believed** spinners help focus showed the same impairment.
  - **Caveat.** Many participants were novice spinner users, so novelty may have contributed.
  - **Sources.** [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/using-fidget-spinners-may-actually-impede-learning); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-using-a-fidget-spinner-can-impair-memory-and-attention/)
- Correlational work links spontaneous fidgeting during lectures with declining retention and mind-wandering over time. Fidgeting there is a **marker** of lapsing attention, not a cure for it. — [Farley, Risko & Kingstone 2013, Frontiers in Psychology, PMC3776418](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3776418/)

### Inferences
- A clickable, draggable smoke effect is a **toy-like, manipulable visual element** on the primary work surface. It works more like a fidget spinner than a game: no goal, no mastery curve. The only direct experimental evidence on that kind of object during an attention task (Soares & Storm) shows **harm** to encoding, and Collins et al. show **no restorative benefit**.
- Novelty effects (Hamari) predict that any engagement value of the smoke will fade over days or weeks. What stays is either a calm ambient visual or a habitual target for idle clicks.
- Streak research is relevant only if the app adds streaks. If it does, design repair or forgiveness (for example "grace days") to avoid the demotivation cliff.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on interactive or animated **decorations** in productivity or focus-timer apps specifically, for example whether animated backgrounds in Pomodoro apps change task performance.
- Isbister and colleagues' HCI work on "fidget widgets" (CHI) was not retrieved in this session. It may give the counterpoint that self-regulatory fidgeting helps some users (for example people with ADHD), but that is **unverified here**.
- ADHD-specific evidence on fidget objects, which is mixed, was not retrieved.

## 4. Habit loops and variable rewards: can simple tactile or visual feedback become compulsive checking?

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed evidence shows that **checking habits** form around content that gives **quick, low-effort rewards**: sessions under 30 seconds, sustained by near-instant reward, and experienced as **annoyance more than addiction**. That mechanism does not need "dopamine content." A highly responsive, satisfying micro-interaction available at all times is a plausible cue–action–reward loop. No study was found that tests a decorative interactive animation specifically. "Dopamine hit" and *Hooked*-style variable-reward rhetoric is mostly popular framing, not direct evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **Oulasvirta, Rattenbury, Ma & Raita 2012, *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* 16(1):105–114 (Finland, Helsinki Institute for Information Technology; 3 studies: logging, diaries/interviews and a controlled field experiment) [field/lab].**
  - Defines the **checking habit** as brief, repetitive inspection of dynamic content that is quickly accessible on the device.
  - A typical check lasts **under 30 seconds** (unlock plus one app), and checks form a notable share of total use.
  - The field experiment suggests checking habits form and persist **because rewards are near-instant**.
  - Checks sometimes act as gateways to other device use.
  - Users described checking as an **annoyance rather than an addiction**.
  - Sources: [Unpaywall/DOI 10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2Fs00779-011-0412-2); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/07/110725101222.htm)
- Later work cites Oulasvirta et al. to argue that cues such as notifications can trigger **automatic** checking. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023, 10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1218886](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1218886/pdf)
- Self-interruptions are costly even before the switch: about 1 s of decision cost, with a pupil-dilation signature (Katidioti et al. 2016, Section 1). A habit that prompts frequent micro-decisions ("touch the smoke or not?") adds that overhead. — [Katidioti et al. 2016](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Katidioti-ComputHumBehav16.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Oulasvirta mechanism needs only (a) a cue, such as boredom, task friction or idle hands on the mouse, (b) a low-effort action, and (c) **immediate** rewarding feedback. A fluid, physics-like smoke reaction to dragging meets (b) and (c). In operant terms, though, it is a **fixed, fully predictable** response. Predictable feedback is generally considered less habit-sticky than variable reward, but **this was not verified with a source** in this session.
- Practical risk: the risk is not addiction. It is **micro self-interruptions at points of task difficulty**, the moments Katidioti & Taatgen found people choose badly, which turn task friction into toy play instead of problem solving.
- **Flag on weak claims.** "Dopamine hits," "dopamine detox" and the *Hooked* model (Eyal 2014, trade book) are **[pop]** framing. No source retrieved here measured dopamine for UI micro-interactions. The defensible, peer-reviewed vocabulary is "checking habit," "self-interruption" and "immediate reward."

### Gaps
- No study was found on whether **non-informational** visual or tactile feedback (no new content, nothing to "check") forms checking habits. Oulasvirta's checks involved dynamic content such as messages and news.
- Peer-reviewed sources on variable-ratio reinforcement in UI design (for example pull-to-refresh) were not retrieved. Eyal and Skinner citations would need separate verification.

## 5. Calm technology, ambient displays and motion guidelines

### Takeaway
Motion is the strongest peripheral attention-capture cue. It is detected better than color or shape in the periphery, and some motion types are measurably more distracting and irritating than others. Calm-technology principles say ambient elements should live in the **periphery** and move to the center only when needed. Accessibility standards require that **auto-moving content can be paused** and that interaction-triggered motion can be disabled (`prefers-reduced-motion`).

### Cited Findings
- **Bartram, Ware & Calvert 2003, *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies* 58:515–545, "Moticons: Detection, distraction and task" (Canada) [RCT/lab].**
  - Simple-motion icons were **better detected and identified than color and shape codes, especially in the periphery**.
  - A second experiment showed that motion type affects both detection and **distraction or irritation**. Some motion types are more distracting than others.
  - The paper gives guidance for choosing motion that minimizes unwanted distraction.
  - The 2001 Interact precursor reports that "certain types of motions" are more distracting and irritating.
  - Sources: [UNH Scholars](https://scholars.unh.edu/ccom/979); [PDF mirror](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Bartram-IJHCS03.pdf); [CCOM Interact 2001](https://ccom.unh.edu/publications/moving-icons-detection-and-distraction)
- **Weiser & Brown (1995/1996), "Designing Calm Technology" (USA, Xerox PARC) [guideline].**
  - Calm technology "informs but doesn't demand our focus or attention." It lives in the **periphery** and moves to the center only when needed.
  - Their example is the PARC "Dangling String," whose motor-driven motion reflected network traffic.
  - The publication year is cited as 1995, 1996 or 1997 across sources.
  - Sources: [calmtech.com reprint](https://calmtech.com/papers/designing-calm-technology); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calm_technology)
- **Amber Case's later "Principles of Calm Technology" (2015) [guideline/pop-practitioner].** They include using the periphery and creating ambient awareness through different senses. — [caseorganic.com](https://caseorganic.com/post/principles-of-calm-technology/)
- **WCAG SC 2.3.3, Animation from Interactions (Level AAA) [guideline].**
  - Motion triggered by interaction should be disableable unless it is essential.
  - Sufficient techniques: CSS `prefers-reduced-motion` (C39), the same query in JavaScript (SCR40), and a user setting that prevents animation.
  - The W3C lists distraction, dizziness, headaches and nausea as vestibular reactions.
  - Source: [W3C Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/animation-from-interactions)
- **WCAG SC 2.2.2, Pause, Stop, Hide (Level A).** Auto-moving content that starts automatically, lasts more than 5 s and appears alongside other content needs a pause, stop or hide mechanism. Deque notes that auto-moving content falls under 2.2.2, separate from 2.3.3. — [Deque 2.3.3 note](https://dequeuniversity.com/resources/wcag2.1/2.3.3-animations-from-interactions); [W3C Understanding 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide)
- A third-party claim that about 35% of adults over 40 have vestibular disorders could **not be verified** against a primary source. Do not use it. — [testparty.ai](https://testparty.ai/blog/wcag-animation-interactions-guide)

### Inferences
- The smoke sits **next to the timer**, which is likely in central or near-peripheral vision while the user works in another window or on paper. Continuous, irregular, high-contrast motion is exactly the kind of signal Bartram et al. found most attention-capturing. Slow, low-amplitude, low-contrast, regular motion (for example a slow "breathing" drift) is the calm-technology-compatible version.
- Under WCAG, ambient smoke that runs continuously should at least respect `prefers-reduced-motion` and offer a pause or static toggle. That is required for Level A conformance (2.2.2) and good practice for AAA (2.3.3).

### Gaps
- No quantitative guideline was found for "safe" animation speed or amplitude for ambient decorations during knowledge work. Bartram et al. compare motion types, but specific parameters would need the full paper.
- No study was found on ambient animated visuals in focus or Pomodoro timers specifically.

## 6. Evidence-based design options for the interactive smoke

### Takeaway
The evidence favors **option B, interactive play only during breaks**, combined with **option C, a calmer, slower, non-interactive smoke during focus blocks**, and a user-controlled reduced-motion or static setting. Keeping the smoke fully interactive during work blocks (option A) has no empirical support as a focus aid. It matches the profile of a fidget-like, self-initiated, same-modality diversion, which the evidence links to worse encoding and higher interruption costs.

### Cited Findings (rationale by option)
- **Against option A (interactive during focus):**
  - Fidget-spinner use during a lecture impaired memory and increased zoning out, even in believers (N = 98 + 48). — [BPS](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/using-fidget-spinners-may-actually-impede-learning)
  - Self-interruptions cost more than external ones (N = 28 + 21). — [Katidioti 2016](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Katidioti-ComputHumBehav16.pdf)
  - Brief task switches did not prevent vigilance decrement (N = 498). — [Helton & Russell 2012](https://www.springermedicine.com/brief-mental-breaks-and-content-free-cues-may-not-keep-you-focus/25692108)
  - Switching to another engaging task restored vigilance less than rest (N = 521). — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf)
  - Checking habits form around near-instant rewards. — [Oulasvirta 2012](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2Fs00779-011-0412-2)
- **For option B (interactive during breaks):**
  - Playful activity during scheduled breaks improves energy and mood (Collins 2019, N = 45; Reinecke 2011, N = 160; Russoniello 2009). — [PMC6670275](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670275); [CAS record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edb,61012079)
  - Micro-breaks improve vigor and fatigue (d ≈ 0.35). — [Albulescu 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)
  - Caveat: a pure toy (fidget spinner) did **not** energize, and the performance benefit of any micro-break after demanding cognitive work is not significant. — [PMC6670275](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6670275); [Albulescu 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)
- **For option C (calmer during focus):**
  - Peripheral motion captures attention, and some motion types distract more. — [Bartram 2003](https://scholars.unh.edu/ccom/979)
  - Calm technology belongs in the periphery. — [Weiser & Brown](https://calmtech.com/papers/designing-calm-technology)
  - WCAG requires pausable auto-motion (2.2.2) and recommends `prefers-reduced-motion` support (2.3.3). — [W3C 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/animation-from-interactions)
- **Break content matters:**
  - Relaxation and social breaks buffered negative affect, while cognitive breaks worsened it (N = 86, Korea). — [Kim et al. 2017](https://metatoc.com/papers/51909-micro-break-activities-at-work-to-recover-from-daily-work-demands)
  - Phone breaks recovered least (N = 414). — [Kang & Kurtzberg 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7044622)
  - Rest beat switching tasks. — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf)

### Inferences (concrete design options)
1. **Recommended default: "Calm in focus, playful in break."**
   - **Focus blocks.** Make the smoke non-interactive, or have it ignore pointer input. Use slow, low-amplitude, low-contrast motion with a regular rhythm and no sudden bursts or high-frequency flicker.
   - **Breaks.** Unlock interaction: drag, click and swirl. Optionally add a gentle visual cue at break start ("the smoke is yours now"), which turns play into a **planned, externally cued** diversion rather than a self-interruption.
2. **Break-play should not crowd out rest.** Offer the toy as an *option* inside the break, alongside a nudge to look away, stand or breathe. Rest and relaxation have the strongest evidence. Toy play is at best neutral to mildly mood-positive. Consider auto-dimming the smoke in the last part of a break so the user is not pulled into screen engagement.
3. **User control and accessibility (needed in any option).** Respect OS `prefers-reduced-motion` with a static or near-static smoke. Provide an in-app toggle (animated / calm / static) and a pause control, which WCAG 2.2.2 requires for auto-moving content.
4. **If option A is kept for users who want it:** make it **opt-in**, not default. Dampen responses during focus (high viscosity, short reaction, no satisfying "swirl" payoff) to reduce immediate-reward reinforcement. Consider logging the number of focus-time interactions as a self-awareness metric. A spike in interactions may mark moments of task friction.
5. **Evaluate in-app (A/B).** Compare (A) and (B+C) on completed focus blocks, self-reported focus, number of focus-block interactions, and number of mid-block app switches. Watch for novelty effects (Hamari 2014) by measuring over 2–4 weeks, not just days.
6. **Avoid adding variable or "surprise" rewards** to the smoke (random particles, rare effects) during focus. That would add a variable-reward element on top of immediate feedback. This rests on general reinforcement reasoning, not a verified source in this session; see Gaps.

### Gaps
- No direct experimental test exists for any of the three options in a timer or productivity app. The recommendations are extrapolations from vigilance, interruption, fidget, break and motion-perception studies.
- The benefit of interactive smoke during breaks has not been tested. The closest analog (a fidget spinner as break activity) showed no energizing effect, so option B may be roughly neutral rather than restorative. Its main value may be delight and brand, not recovery.
- Individual differences (ADHD, anxiety, users who self-regulate by fidgeting) were not covered with verified sources. Some users may genuinely benefit from a fidget outlet during focus, which argues for an opt-in toggle rather than a hard ban.
