# Fidgeting, doodling and small repetitive motor activity: help or hurt attention?

Context: a focus-timer app has interactive animated "smoke" around the timer that the user can click and drag during a work block. Is this like fidgeting (possibly helpful) or a distraction?

---

## 1. Fidget spinners, fidget cubes and stress balls: controlled studies

### Takeaway
The controlled evidence is negative to null. Fidget spinners impaired lecture memory in undergraduates (US, n = 98 + 48) and worsened observed attention in young children with ADHD (US, n = 60). A 2025 meta-analysis of group-design classroom studies (10 studies, N = 509) found an effect of essentially zero (Hedges' g = -0.014). The only positive signals come from tiny single-case studies.

### Cited Findings
- **Soares & Storm (2020), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 34(1), 277-284, "Putting a negative spin on it" (US, Mississippi State University).** Exp. 1: 98 undergraduates watched a 15-min video lecture on bread baking, either using a fidget spinner or not. Half of the non-users sat next to a spinner user. Spinner users answered significantly fewer test questions correctly. They also reported more "zoning out" and more difficulty staying on task. Sitting next to a spinner user had no detectable effect. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-using-a-fidget-spinner-can-impair-memory-and-attention/); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/using-fidget-spinners-may-actually-impede-learning); [Soares CV (affiliation)](https://psychology.msstate.edu/sites/www.psychology.msstate.edu/files/2024-11/Soares%20CV%20September%202020.pdf)
- Soares & Storm Exp. 2 recruited 48 undergraduates who *believed* spinners help them focus. The memory cost replicated, so the harm is not explained by negative expectations. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-using-a-fidget-spinner-can-impair-memory-and-attention/)
- The authors' own caveat: many participants were using a spinner for the first time, so novelty may have inflated the cost. Habitual users might differ. They do not recommend banning spinners. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-using-a-fidget-spinner-can-impair-memory-and-attention/)
- **Graziano, Garcia & Landis (2020), *Journal of Attention Disorders* 24(1), 163-171 (US, Florida International University; online-first 2018).** A-B-A-B classroom design with 60 children with ADHD (mean age 4.86 years, 83% Hispanic). Four children per classroom received spinners on 3 days (n = 48). Attention was coded from 5-min classroom videos. Spinner use was linked to lower activity level only in the first treatment phase, and to *worse attention in both phases*, even inside an evidence-based classroom intervention. Peers did not appear to be disturbed. — [FIU Discovery record](https://discovery.fiu.edu/display/pub76375); [CHADD summary](https://chadd.org/attention-article/sensory-classroom-interventions/); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/3-studies-argue-against-fidget-spinners-in-the-classroom/)
- Critique of Graziano et al.: 60 children is too few to look for subgroups who might benefit, so one study should not be over-generalized. — [Learning and the Brain blog (A. Watson)](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/do-fidget-spinners-help-children-with-adhd-2/)
- **Schoenen, Alsip, Martinez, Grekov, Aspiranti & Hulac (2025), *School Psychology Review* 54(3), 315-327 (US).** Meta-analysis of group-design classroom studies of fidget devices: 10 studies, N = 509. Overall Hedges' **g = -0.014** (range -2.98 to 1.15), with no significant moderators. Conclusion: benefits for academics or behavior are "negligible" in large groups. — [University of Kentucky record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-fidget-devices-as-academic-and-behavioral-inte/)
- **The same group's 2024 single-case meta-analysis** (10 studies, 59 students) found an overall positive effect (LRR = 0.32). Single-case designs are weaker evidence and are prone to publication bias. — [University of Kentucky record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/fidget-devices-as-academic-and-behavioral-interventions-a-meta-an/)
- **Aspiranti & Hulac (2022), *Behavior Analysis in Practice* 15** (single-case, US): 3 second-graders with ADHD. Spinners showed some positive effect on on-task behavior but **no increase in work completed**. — [PMC9120292](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9120292/); [INSPED summary](https://www.insped.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/fidget-toys.pdf)
- Stress balls versus spinners in college students (Hulac et al.): no significant differences across no-fidget, spinner and stress-ball conditions. A critical narrative review (Kriescher et al., 2023) found no evidence that fidget toys improve attention or behavior. Both claims come from secondary summaries. — [INSPED fidget-toys summary](https://www.insped.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/fidget-toys.pdf)
- A classroom study of 233 children by Driesen et al. compared fidget spinners and bouncy bands on maths and listening. Details were not retrieved. — [INSPED summary](https://www.insped.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/fidget-toys.pdf)

### Inferences
- A fidget spinner is partly a *visual* object: it spins, wobbles and invites watching. That may explain why it hurts while simpler tactile objects look more neutral. This is my inference and was not directly tested in these studies.
- Popular marketing ("fidget toys help ADHD, autism, anxiety focus") is **not supported** by controlled group studies. Treat it as a popular claim.

### Gaps
- Soares & Storm effect sizes (Cohen's d, means/SDs) were not retrievable from accessible sources. Only the direction and significance are confirmed.
- I found no well-powered RCT on fidget *cubes* specifically. The stress-ball evidence is limited to secondary summaries of small studies.
- Almost no studies test adults doing self-paced knowledge work (writing, coding) rather than listening to lectures.

## 2. Doodling: Andrade (2010), replications and failures, free versus structured doodling

### Takeaway
Andrade's well-known "+29% recall" came from a tiny UK study (n = 40, 2.5-min task) that used *structured shading of printed shapes*, not free doodling. A larger, preregistered-style Canadian replication (Spencer-Mueller & Fenske, 2024, N = 222) found **no benefit**, with Bayesian evidence for the null. Unstructured or free doodling tends to be neutral or worse (Boggs et al., 2017; Xie et al., 2026). Deliberate, content-related drawing *does* help memory, but that is a different mechanism (the "drawing effect") and not fidgeting.

### Cited Findings
- **Andrade (2010), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 24(1), 100-106 (UK, Plymouth / MRC APU Cambridge panel).** 40 adults aged 18-55, randomized 20/20. They listened to a dull 2.5-min mock phone message and wrote down the names of party-goers. The "doodling" group shaded printed squares and circles (mean 36.3 shapes shaded, range 3-110). Results: monitoring was 7.7 vs 6.9 correct (Mann-Whitney U = 124, p = .01, one-tailed). Surprise recall was 7.5 vs 5.8 items (**29% more**), F(1,38) = 6.0, p = .02. With monitoring as a covariate, the group effect became marginal (F(1,37) = 3.8, p = .058). The proposed mechanisms were reduced daydreaming or arousal regulation, but *neither was measured*. Participants were deliberately tested when tired and told the tape was dull, which maximized boredom. — [Andrade author manuscript (Plymouth PEARL)](https://pure.plymouth.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/39086792/Andrade_Doodling%20author%20version.pdf)
- **Spencer-Mueller & Fenske (2024), *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* 77(8), 1780-1796 (Canada, University of Guelph).** Exp. 1 (N = 50) closely replicated Andrade after a 15-min boredom induction. Memory showed no difference (F(1,48) = .55, p = .46, ηp² = .01), and doodlers were nominally *lower*. The Bayes factor strongly favored the null. Exp. 2 (N = 172, 45-min lecture) compared listen-only, note-taking, structured doodling and unstructured doodling (n = 43 each). Note-takers outperformed listen-only (d = .75), structured doodlers (d = .95) and unstructured doodlers (d = 1.03). **Neither doodling condition differed from listen-only.** Doodling did not reduce boredom or mind-wandering. Note-takers mind-wandered less (d ≈ -.8 vs every other group). — [PMC11295400](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295400)
- **Boggs, Cohen & Marchand (2017), *Psychological Thought* 10(1), 206-216 (US).** 93 undergraduates heard an earthquake conversation under one of four conditions: structured doodling, unstructured doodling, note-taking or control. **Unstructured doodlers scored significantly lower than structured doodlers and note-takers.** Doodling did not beat listening alone, while note-taking did help. — [UNLV repository](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/edpsych_fac_articles/164); [PsychOpen news](https://www.psychopen.eu/news/article/note-taking-is-more-effective-for-recall-ability-than-doodling/)
- A press account says a follow-up in which people doodled only when they wanted to found that free doodlers outperformed controls. This claim is unverified and comes from a secondary source only. — [St. Thomas Newsroom](https://news.stthomas.edu/doodlers-beware/)
- **Xie, Xu, Gao & Yan (2026), *Scandinavian Journal of Psychology* 67(3), 898-908 (China, East China Normal University).** Between-subjects design: structured, unstructured or no doodling, crossed with high or low boredom. Under high boredom, the *unstructured* doodling group's recall declined significantly. Recall also got worse as drawings became more complex and polished. Only the abstract was available. — [ECNU record](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/effects-of-doodling-style-boredom-and-sensation-seeking-on-recall/)
- Student and unpublished replications of Andrade (Stockton University; University of St. Thomas) found null or opposite results. They are weak evidence. — [Stockton thesis PDF](https://www.stockton.edu/social-behavioral-sciences/documents/psyc_distinction_projects/Casario.pdf); [St. Thomas Newsroom](https://news.stthomas.edu/doodlers-beware/)
- **Drawing effect (Wammes, Meade & Fernandes, 2016, QJEP; review 2019, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*; Canada, Waterloo).** Drawing the *to-be-remembered content* (for example, drawing "apple" for 40 s) often more than doubled recall versus writing it. This is semantic, elaborative encoding, not off-task doodling. — [U Waterloo news](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/news/need-remember-something-better-draw-it-study-finds); [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/for-learning-drawing-a-picture-may-really-be-worth-a-thousand-words.html)

### Inferences
- Applying the standard approximation d ≈ 2·√(F/df), Andrade's recall effect is about **d ≈ 0.8** with very wide confidence intervals (n = 20 per group). An effect that large from n = 40 that then fails a larger replication fits the pattern of an inflated original estimate. This is my calculation, not one reported by the paper.
- If doodling helps at all, the helpful version is *simple, repetitive and low-skill* (shading). Free, elaborate or "polished" doodling looks neutral to harmful (Boggs; Xie). That pattern supports the "low attentional demand" hypothesis in Q5.
- The note "Meade, Wammes & Fernandes 2019 drawing vs doodling" in the brief most likely refers to the 2019 *Current Directions* review of the drawing effect. I found no 2019 paper by them directly comparing drawing with doodling.

### Gaps
- Exact statistics for Boggs et al. (2017) were not retrieved beyond the direction of effects.
- I found no study of "digital doodling" (dragging on screen) as a secondary task during work.
- I found no meta-analysis of doodling and recall.

## 3. Movement and ADHD: Sarver (2015), Hartanto (2016), Kercood and Grskovic; optimal-stimulation theory

### Takeaway
In children with ADHD, more spontaneous gross-motor movement *co-occurs* with better working-memory and flanker performance. In typically developing (TD) children it is neutral or slightly harmful. This supports a "compensatory movement" model, but the studies are small and correlational, and none of them involved adults or screen-based fidgeting. Fine-motor tactile objects showed modest benefits only in tiny single-case studies.

### Cited Findings
- **Sarver, Rapport, Kofler, Raiker & Friedman (2015), *Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology* 43(7), 1219-1232 (US, Children's Learning Clinic, University of Central Florida).** 52 boys aged 8-12 (ADHD n = 29, TD n = 23). They did four counterbalanced phonological working-memory tasks across four sessions, with gross-motor activity coded from video. In the ADHD group, higher activity predicted **significantly better, but not normalized**, working-memory performance. In TD children, higher activity predicted *somewhat lower* working-memory performance. **Movement did not predict attention in either group.** At the individual level, children with ADHD were more often "reliably Improved" and TD children more often "Deteriorated" at their highest versus lowest activity level. The authors stress that the design is non-experimental, so no causal claims can be made, and call for replication. — [Sarver et al. 2015 PDF (FSU CLC)](https://psy.fsu.edu/clc/Publications/CLC_Sarver2015.pdf); [UCF STARS record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/266)
- Press coverage ("ADHD children learn better the more they move") overstated these correlational findings. — [UMMC news](https://umc.edu/news/Miscellaneous/2015/June/ADHD-CHILDREN-LEARN-BETTER-THE-MORE-THEY-MOVE--STUDY-SUGGESTS.html)
- **Hartanto, Krafft, Iosif & Schweitzer (2016), *Child Neuropsychology* 22 (online 2015) (US, UC Davis MIND Institute).** 44 children aged 10-17 with ADHD or TD wore an ankle actometer during a flanker task, analyzed trial by trial. There was a significant group × performance interaction: **more intense movement went with better performance in ADHD, not in TD.** Children with ADHD moved more intensely on correct trials than on error trials, and TD children showed no difference. — [PMC4675699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4675699)
- **Kercood, Grskovic, Lee & Emmert (2007), *Journal of Behavioral Education* 16(4), 303-310 (US).** 8 fourth- and fifth-graders with attention problems did math word problems in 20-min sessions, with or without manipulating a small tactile object. The object **reduced excessive movement and increased task completion**. — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-fine-motor-movement-and-tactile-stimulation-on-the/)
- **Emmert, Kercood & Grskovic (2009), JAASEP:** tactile versus auditory stimulation versus student choice. Students attempted and solved slightly more problems and showed less off-task behavior, but effects were "very modest". — [JAASEP Winter 2009](https://media.naset.com/fileadmin/user_upload/JAASEP/JAASEP_Winter_2009.pdf); [AASEP article](https://www.aasep.org/article/view/65)
- Theoretical frame: Sarver et al. contrast a "deficit" model, where hyperactivity impedes learning, with "compensatory" models, where movement raises under-aroused cortical activity to support executive functioning. Their data fit the compensatory account. — [Sarver et al. 2015 PDF](https://psy.fsu.edu/clc/Publications/CLC_Sarver2015.pdf)

### Inferences
- The ADHD benefit concerns *gross-motor, self-generated, non-visual* movement such as leg swinging and seat movement during demanding working-memory tasks. Typically developing participants show no benefit or a small cost (Sarver). A typical focus-app user is probably closer to the TD profile unless they have ADHD.
- None of these studies show that a *screen-based visual* activity provides the same compensatory stimulation. Gross-motor movement leaves the eyes and hands free for the main task, while dragging smoke on screen does not.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve primary sources on Zentall's optimal-stimulation theory (Zentall & Zentall, 1983 and later work), so it is not cited here with specifics.
- I found no adult ADHD experiments of controlled fidgeting during knowledge work.
- Sarver and Hartanto effect sizes were not extracted (Sarver reports mixed-model results and reliable-change classifications).

## 4. Spontaneous fidgeting as a marker of mind-wandering and low attention

### Takeaway
In typical adults, spontaneous fidgeting *rises* when the mind wanders and as time on task increases, and it predicts *worse* retention. Fidgeting is better read as a symptom of attentional disengagement or boredom than as a cure for it.

### Cited Findings
- **Seli, Carriere, Thomson, Cheyne, Martens & Smilek (2014), "Restless mind, restless body," *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 40(3), 660-668 (Canada, University of Waterloo).** Participants did a metronome response task with thought probes while sitting on a Wii Balance Board that measured fidgeting. Study 1: mind-wandering periods showed **more fidgeting** and more response variability than on-task periods. Study 2: only *deep* mind wandering increased fidgeting, while mild mind wandering already increased response variability. — [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1330885)
- **Carriere, Seli & Smilek (2013), "Wandering in both mind and body," *Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology* 67(1), 19-31 (Canada).** Trait-level everyday mind-wandering and inattention were linked to the tendency to fidget spontaneously. — [Duke FDS record](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/pn/faculty/pjs48/publications/335745)
- **Farley, Risko & Kingstone (2013), *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:619 (Canada, UBC).** 21 undergraduates watched a 40-min lecture video while filmed, rating attention every 5 min. Attention declined over time (β = -0.18). Macro-fidgeting increased over time (β = 0.30). Macro-fidgeting predicted **lower retention** (β = -0.10, p < .01), and this held when attention was controlled (β = -0.09). Micro-fidgeting showed a weaker effect (β = -0.05). The authors propose a speculative stress-based account. The sample is very small. — [PMC3776418](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3776418/)

### Inferences
- For typical users, a rise in fidget-like interaction such as clicking or dragging the smoke during a work block is more plausibly a *sign* of mind-wandering than a tool against it. The app could treat frequent smoke interaction as a soft signal of disengagement. This is a design inference that has not been tested.

### Gaps
- Seli et al. (2014) sample sizes and effect sizes were not retrieved.
- I found no studies of digital or mouse "fidgeting" (idle cursor movement, clicking) as a mind-wandering marker. This literature likely exists in HCI but was not searched within the budget.

## 5. Low-attention (automatic motor) versus attention-demanding (visual, game-like) fidgets

### Takeaway
The pattern across studies suggests that harmless or helpful secondary activity is **simple, repetitive, tactile or motor, needs no visual monitoring, and offers no reward or progression** (for example, shading shapes, leg movement, a squeezed object). Activities with visual feedback, novelty, complexity or aesthetic goals (spinners, elaborate doodles) tend to impair memory. No study directly manipulated "visual/game-like" versus "automatic motor" fidgeting, so this conclusion is inferential.

### Cited Findings
- Andrade deliberately chose an *auditory* primary task so that doodling would compete minimally for modality-specific resources. She also chose *simple shading* so that it would be done "absent-mindedly". Even so, the benefit did not replicate. — [Andrade 2010 manuscript](https://pure.plymouth.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/39086792/Andrade_Doodling%20author%20version.pdf); [Spencer-Mueller & Fenske 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295400)
- More complex and polished doodles were associated with worse recall, and unstructured doodling hurt under high boredom. — [Xie et al. 2026 (ECNU)](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/effects-of-doodling-style-boredom-and-sensation-seeking-on-recall/)
- Unstructured doodling scored lower than structured doodling. — [Boggs et al. 2017](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/edpsych_fac_articles/164)
- Fidget spinners, which produce a visually engaging spinning motion, increased self-reported zoning out and reduced recall, even in believers. — [PsyPost on Soares & Storm 2020](https://www.psypost.org/study-finds-using-a-fidget-spinner-can-impair-memory-and-attention/)
- Tactile objects with no visual component reduced excess movement and slightly increased task completion in children with attention problems (n = 8). — [Kercood et al. 2007](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-fine-motor-movement-and-tactile-stimulation-on-the/)
- Note-taking, a *task-related* secondary activity, beat all doodling conditions on recall, attention and mind-wandering (d ≈ 0.75-1.03 on recall). — [Spencer-Mueller & Fenske 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295400)

### Inferences (applied to the "interactive smoke" feature)
- Clicking and dragging animated smoke is **visual, screen-based, uses the same hand and mouse as the work, gives responsive aesthetic feedback, and is novel**. On every one of these dimensions it resembles the fidget spinner (harmful) more than shading or tactile squeezing (neutral or possibly helpful).
- In visual or screen work such as reading, writing or coding, the smoke competes directly for the visual channel and for the input device. That is a worse case than Andrade's auditory-primary setup. Its expected effect is a *cost*, not a benefit.
- The benefit claimed for ADHD comes from gross-motor or tactile movement and does not transfer to on-screen visual play. There is no evidence that screen fidgeting helps people with ADHD.
- Design options consistent with the evidence: make smoke interaction available only during breaks; or make it ambient and non-interactive during focus blocks; or, if it stays interactive, keep it minimal (no rewards, no novelty, slow and subtle response). Frequent interaction could be logged as a possible mind-wandering signal (Q4).

### Gaps
- There is no direct experiment comparing visual or game-like screen fidgets with automatic motor fidgets in adults during knowledge work.
- I found no studies on "calm tech" ambient animations or interactive screensavers and their effect on focus-session performance.
- Novelty may wear off over time (Soares & Storm's caveat), and no longitudinal data on habituated fidget use were found.
