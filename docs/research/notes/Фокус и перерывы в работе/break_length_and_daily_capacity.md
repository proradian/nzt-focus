# Break Length (10 vs 15 min), Long-Break Counting, and Daily Deep-Work Capacity

Scope note: notes from the October 2026 follow-up search behind three app decisions: (1) why the regular break is 10 minutes and when it should be 15; (2) when a long break is due; (3) whether "4 hours of deep work a day" is a real limit. Peer-reviewed sources are marked [PR]; vendor and pop-science sources are marked [NON-PR]. Every claim has an inline source.

Verification: in this session, PubMed, J-STAGE and Frontiers were blocked by the network policy. **Every new source below was checked against its abstract or search-result summaries only, not the full text.** In [SOURCES.md](../../SOURCES.md) these are marked [А].

## Q1. Is a 15-minute break better than 10 minutes after a 60-minute block?

### Takeaway
**No study directly compares 10- and 15-minute breaks after an hour of cognitive work.** The evidence is indirect and leans slightly towards 15 minutes once the working day exceeds about 2 hours. It does not show that 15 minutes is optimal. Objective markers of fatigue outlast both 10 and 15 minutes. Longer breaks bring smaller extra gains for each added minute.

### Cited Findings

**Horie (1987), 「VDT作業における一連続作業時間と休憩に関する研究」, *人間工学 (Japanese Journal of Ergonomics)* 23(6):373–383, DOI [10.5100/jje.23.373](https://doi.org/10.5100/jje.23.373) [PR, Japan, Nihon University]**
- Task: single-digit search and addition on a VDT. Outcomes: physiological and psychological responses and work efficiency.
- Abstract: the combination of **60 min continuous work + 10 min break** gave the best results. **For more than 2 hours of VDT work a day, a break of 15 min or longer after every 60 min** is desirable. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jje1965/23/6/23_6_373/_article/-char/ja/)
- Sample size and the full list of conditions were not in the abstract. Not verified.
- Inference: this looks like the origin of the MHLW "10–15 min" range, which would mean the range is conditional on daily load rather than a free choice. This link is not stated in the MHLW guideline itself.

**Japanese follow-up on break content (1995/2010s), 「VDT作業の休憩時間の過ごし方に関する一考察」, *日本経営工学会誌* 46(3) [PR, Japan]**
- 60 min work, then a 15 min break spent one of three ways: 15 min eyes-closed rest; 5 min physical load (Harvard step test); 10 min mental load (figure division test). Light physical activity was more effective than eyes-closed rest. 8 male students. — [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jimapre/46/3/46_KJ00001733828/_article/-char/ja/)

**Brazaitis et al. (2023), "Regular short-duration breaks do not prevent mental fatigue and decline in cognitive efficiency in healthy young men during an office-like simulated mental working day: An EEG study," *International Journal of Psychophysiology* [PR, Lithuania, Lithuanian Sports University]**
- 18 men aged 23–29, 7 hours of computer office-like tasks, **10-min break every 50 min**.
- Tasks demanding high mental effort deteriorated over the day despite the breaks. Cognitive function had not fully recovered even after **4.5 h of rest**. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167876023000612)
- No 15-min condition was tested, so this shows that "10 may not be enough over a long day", not that "15 is better".
- Press coverage misreported the study as "breaks cause exhaustion". [Full Fact](https://fullfact.org/online/short-breaks-study-false/) showed this was wrong.

**Jacquet, Poulin-Charronnat, Bard & Lepers (2021), "Persistence of Mental Fatigue on Motor Control," *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:588253, DOI [10.3389/fpsyg.2020.588253](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.588253) [PR, France]**
- 15 participants, 32-min demanding cognitive task (TLDB), measured immediately after and at 10 and 20 min of recovery. — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.588253/pdf)
- Subjective fatigue partly recovered over 20 min but did not return to baseline. EEG theta/alpha power rose and arm-pointing movement duration kept increasing during recovery. **Objective markers showed fatigue persisting for at least 20 min.**
- Implication: self-report runs ahead of objective recovery. A "felt fine" signal should not be used to shorten breaks.
- The same paper cites Rivers (1896): 30 min of total rest did not neutralise the effects of 30 min of mental work, and 60 min only partly did. Not verified.

**Lim & Kwok (2016), "The Effects of Varying Break Length on Attention and Time on Task," *Human Factors* 58(3), DOI [10.1177/0018720815617395](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720815617395) [PR, Singapore]**
- 71 participants, 1-hour auditory oddball task, two rests of **1, 5 or 10 min**.
- Reaction-time improvement after the break grew with break length. But **longer breaks were followed by steeper decline** in the next block. The authors read this as effort allocation: after a long rest people push harder before their resources have fully recovered. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283243253_The_Effects_of_Varying_Break_Length_on_Attention_and_Time_on_Task)
- A companion study, Lim, Teng, Wong & Chee (2016), *NeuroImage* 134:64–73, used the same break lengths with fMRI. Not read.

**Albulescu et al. (2022), *PLOS ONE* (already in the main report) [PR]**
- Break lengths in the meta-analysis ranged from 8 s to 10 min. Performance gains grew with break length, while vigor and fatigue effects did not depend on length. The authors suggest that recovery after depleting work may need more than 10 min. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)

**Dababneh, Swanson & Shell (2001), *Ergonomics* 44(2):164–174 [PR, USA]**
- 30 meat-processing workers. Both schedules added 36 min of break time a day: 12 × 3 min or 4 × 9 min. Neither cut production. **Workers preferred 9-min breaks**, and the 9-min schedule reduced leg discomfort more. — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/198823)

**Henning et al. (1997)** (already in the main report): operators preferred 3-min pauses to 30-s ones. **Göksu et al. (2026)** (already in the main report): students called 3-min breaks too short and asked for longer, rarer ones.

**OSHA 3092 (1997), citing NIOSH** (already in SOURCES): 15 min after 1 hour of intense visual or repetitive work.

**Blasche et al. (2018), "Comparison of rest-break interventions during a mentally demanding task," *Stress and Health* 34(5):629–638, DOI 10.1002/smi.2830 [PR, Austria]**
- 66 students in 4-hour classes. Compared no break, an unstructured break, and breaks with physical activity or relaxation. Effects were measured up to **20 min after** the break. An unstructured break raised vigor; exercise and relaxation also lowered fatigue. The break length was not in the abstract. — [PMC6585675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6585675)

### Inferences
- The case for 15 minutes is strongest when the day is long: Horie's condition is more than 2 hours a day.
- The case against 15 minutes everywhere: diminishing and delayed returns from longer breaks (Lim & Kwok), and the time cost.
- Design used in NZT Focus: the regular break is **10 min per hour of work, +5 min once the current series has 2+ hours of focus**. The default day becomes 10 → 15 → 25 (long). This is a synthesis and has not been tested as a whole.

### Gaps
- No direct 10-vs-15 comparison on knowledge work.
- Horie 1987: sample and conditions not checked in the full text.

## Q2. 10/10/25 vs 15/15/15: how should the same total rest be split?

### Takeaway
Both give 45 min of rest per 3 hours. Nobody has compared them. The arguments for keeping one long break are partly functional and partly about recovery mechanisms.

### Cited Findings
- **Boucsein & Thum (1997)** (already in SOURCES): before midday, short frequent breaks (7.5 min after 50) relieved strain better. Later in the day, when fatigue had built up, the longer break was better. Length is confounded with frequency, because the long break came after 100 min.
- **Sievertsen, Gino & Piovesan (2016)**, PNAS: test scores fall 0.9% SD per hour of the school day. A **20–30 min break** raises them by 1.7% SD, about two hours of decline. — [PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113)
- **Hunter & Wu (2016)**, *J Appl Psychol*, 95 employees: break length interacted with break frequency. Longer breaks and frequent short breaks were both linked to more resources than infrequent short breaks. Coverage disagrees on whether length had a main effect: [Baylor](https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2015/new-research-confirms-how-take-better-workday-breaks); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835).
- Recovery literature (Sonnentag & Fritz; Trougakos; Bosch; already in the main report): detachment, meals, going outside and social contact need more than 15 min. The MHLW nap advice (15–20 min, at most 30) does not fit into a 15-min break.
- Jacquet et al. (Q1): objective fatigue persists for ≥ 20 min, so only the long break approaches full recovery.

### Inferences
- Keep the long break. Grow the regular break with accumulated load instead of making all breaks equal.

## Q3. When is a long break due? Counting blocks vs. real rest

### Takeaway
A pause between blocks at least as long as the long break does the same recovery job as the long break. Counting blocks by calendar day gave a 25-min break after a 3-hour lunch.

### Cited Findings
- Sievertsen 2016 (Q2): a 20–30 min break offsets about 2 hours of decline.
- Jacquet 2021 (Q1): fatigue markers persist for about 20 min.
- **Tucker, Folkard & Macdonald (2003)**, *Lancet* (already in SOURCES): injury risk rose between breaks within 2-hour work spells. **Tucker et al. (2006)**: risk rose from the first to the second half-hour after a break, then stayed flat. — [Circadian summary](https://circadian.com/q-a-how-often-should-shift-workers-take-breaks)
- **Monk, Trafton & Boehm-Davis (2008)** (already in the fidgeting notes): longer interruptions mean longer resumption lags, with the effect levelling off. This concerns interruptions in the middle of a task. It matters little for a break at a natural stopping point with a "where I stopped" note.

### Inferences (implemented)
- A pause of ≥ `longMin` (25 min by default) between the end of one block and the start of the next resets the series.
- A block counts towards the long break if it lasted at least half its plan. Its minutes always count towards the 2-hour threshold.
- The series is not reset at midnight: the night is already a long pause.

## Q4. Is "4 hours of deep work a day" a real limit?

### Takeaway
**No study found a hard daily limit.** The 4-hour figure comes from Ericsson et al. (1993) and from the MHLW note citing an unnamed study. What has been measured is a **gradual decline that hits demanding tasks and decisions hardest**. Easy and routine work holds up better.

### Cited Findings
- **Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993)**: diaries of 10 + 10 violinists at one Berlin academy, plus anecdotes about writers and scientists. Popularized by Cal Newport (*Deep Work*) as "about 4 hours". **Macnamara & Maitra (2019)** re-ran the design with 39 violinists and did not replicate the core finding. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/beautiful-minds/practice-alone-does-not-make-perfect-studies-find/); [UNC review](https://pitjournal.unc.edu/2023/01/05/building-an-expert-the-deliberate-practice-debate-in-music-performance/)
- **Brazaitis et al. (2023)** (Q1): over a 7-hour day, tasks demanding high mental effort deteriorated specifically.
- **Wiehler, Branzoli, Adanyeguh, Mochel & Pessiglione (2022)**, "A neuro-metabolic account of why daylong cognitive work alters the control of economic decisions," *Current Biology* [PR, France]: 40 participants did **6+ hours** of hard or easy cognitive work. After hard work, glutamate rose in the lateral prefrontal cortex, and choices shifted towards low-effort, short-delay options. — [HAL](https://hal.sorbonne-universite.fr/hal-04534917); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/2022/09/neuroimaging-study-suggests-mental-fatigue-helps-preserve-the-chemical-integrity-of-the-brain-63926)
- **Linder et al. (2014)**, "Time of Day and the Decision to Prescribe Antibiotics," *JAMA Intern Med* 174(12):2029–31 [PR, USA]: 21,867 acute respiratory infection visits in 23 practices. Antibiotic prescribing rose across each 4-hour clinic session, and doctors were **26% more likely to prescribe in the 4th hour than in the 1st**. This was observational. — [AHRQ PSNet](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/time-day-and-decision-prescribe-antibiotics)
- **Collewet & Sauermann (2017)**, "Working Hours and Productivity," *Labour Economics* 47:96–106 [PR, Netherlands]: in call-centre agents, average handling time per call rose with hours worked, even among mostly part-time staff. Within-person variation came from central scheduling. — [SOFI WP](https://swopec.hhs.se/sofiwp/abs/sofiwp2017_003.htm)
- **Sievertsen et al. (2016)** (Q2): −0.9% SD per hour, a small but steady slope with no cliff.
- **Pencavel (2014/2015)** (already in `break_schedules_pomodoro.md`): weekly output is proportional to hours up to about 48–50 h, then rises at a decreasing rate. This is factory work. — [IZA DP 8129](https://docs.iza.org/dp8129.pdf)
- [NON-PR] Vendor claims: tracked deep work of 1–1.5 h/day (Make10000Hours) and "51% of time in deep-work tools" (Reclaim.ai). These are unverified and not used.

### Inferences (implemented)
- Keep about 4 hours as a soft marker, not a stop sign. After 4 hours, the app suggests moving to easier work (email, routine) or finishing, rather than "stop now".
- Put the hardest work in the first blocks of the day.

### Gaps
- No field study measures daily capacity for self-paced knowledge work.
- Individual differences are large. Per-user data from the app could show a personal curve.
