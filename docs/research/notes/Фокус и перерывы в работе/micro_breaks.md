# Micro-breaks (seconds to ~10 min) during focused work: evidence for a focus app with ~1-hour sessions

## 1. Meta-analysis: Albulescu et al. 2022 (PLOS ONE, Romania). Do micro-breaks improve well-being and performance? Does it depend on break length or task type?

### Takeaway
Micro-breaks reliably give small-to-moderate gains in well-being (vigor up, fatigue down, d of about 0.35). For performance the overall effect is small and not significant (d = 0.16). Performance gains do appear with longer breaks and on clerical or creative tasks, but not on cognitively demanding tasks. Micro-breaks never made performance worse.

### Cited Findings
- Authors are at the Department of Psychology, West University of Timișoara, Romania. The title is "'Give me a break!' A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance", PLOS ONE 2022. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460); [PMC9432722](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9432722/)
- **Sample:** 19 publications, 22 independent samples, N = 2,335, mean age 31.2. Eleven samples were students, ten were employees and one was "normal volunteers". Break lengths in the included studies ranged from **8 seconds to 10 minutes**. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Vigor:** d = 0.36, 95% CI [0.16, 0.55], p < .001 (k = 9, n = 913). — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Fatigue (reduction):** d = 0.35, 95% CI [0.19, 0.50], p < .001 (k = 9, n = 803). Heterogeneity was negligible after removing one outlier. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Performance:** d = 0.16, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.37], not significant (k = 15, n = 1,132). The paper is internally inconsistent here: the abstract gives p = .116 and the results section gives p = .17. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Break duration moderator (performance):** in meta-regression, longer breaks went with larger performance gains (b = 0.07, p = .006, R² = .34). No moderator, duration included, was significant for vigor or fatigue. No cutoff analysis (for example "best at X min") was reported. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Task type moderator (performance):** Q(2) = 6.53, p = .011.
  - Cognitive tasks: d = -0.09, 95% CI [-0.39, 0.30], not significant.
  - Creative tasks: d = 0.38, 95% CI [0.11, 0.64], p = .006.
  - Clerical tasks: d = 0.56, 95% CI [0.01, 1.12], p = .047, based on only 2 studies.
  — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Break activity:** too heterogeneous to test as a moderator. Activities fell into cognitive (5 studies), physical (6), relaxing (6) and mixed (5). — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Limitations reported by the authors:**
  - Few studies per outcome.
  - Poor reliability reporting for performance measures.
  - Self-reported well-being.
  - Only vigor and fatigue were examined.
  - Possible publication bias for performance (Egger p = .048). Trim-and-fill did not change the estimate materially.
  — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- The meta-analysis uses the conventional cap of **<10 min** for a micro-break, which later reviews ground in interviews by Bennett et al. 2020. — [Bennett, Gabriel & Calderwood 2020, JOHP (abstract)](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/examining-the-interplay-of-micro-break-durations-and-activities-f/)

### Inferences
- For an app, the strongest evidence-based promise is "you will feel less tired and more energetic". It is not "you will be more productive", which holds only for routine or creative work.
- The positive duration–performance slope inside the 8 s–10 min range suggests that, if performance matters, breaks nearer several minutes beat breaks of a few seconds.
- The null result for cognitively demanding tasks suggests a deep-work user may see no output gain. They also lose nothing, since breaks did not hurt performance.

### Gaps
- No optimal frequency or interval was estimated. No dose–response for vigor or fatigue was found.
- Activity type could not be compared meta-analytically.

## 2. Key primary studies: Ariga & Lleras 2011; Kim, Park & Niu 2017; Henning et al. 1997; Galinsky et al. 2000/2007 (NIOSH); McLean et al. 2001

### Takeaway
The field studies of computer work (Henning, Galinsky, McLean) agree on the main point. Adding frequent short breaks of 30 s to 5 min, roughly hourly or more often, reduces discomfort and eyestrain and **does not reduce output**, even though work time is shorter. Lab evidence (Ariga & Lleras) suggests even two very brief task switches can prevent the vigilance decrement over 50 min. Diary evidence (Kim et al.) favours relaxation and social micro-breaks over cognitive ones.

### Cited Findings
**Ariga & Lleras 2011, *Cognition* 118(3):439–443 (USA, University of Illinois)**
- **Theory ("goal habituation"):** the vigilance decrement happens because cognitive control fails to keep the task goal active over long periods. Briefly deactivating and then reactivating the goal by switching tasks prevents it. — [PubMed 21211793](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/)
- **Design:** N = 84 across 4 groups, on a **50-minute** repetitive computer vigilance task.
  - Control: no diversions.
  - "Switch": memorised 4 digits, saw them twice during the task and responded, so had **2 brief diversions**.
  - "No-switch": memorised the digits but never saw them.
  - "Digit-ignored": saw the digits but was told to ignore them.
  — [U. of Illinois News Bureau](https://news.illinois.edu/brief-diversions-vastly-improve-focus-researchers-find/)
- **Result:** the switch group showed no performance decline. All the other groups declined significantly. Numeric effect sizes were not available in the sources reviewed. — [U. of Illinois News Bureau](https://news.illinois.edu/brief-diversions-vastly-improve-focus-researchers-find/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/)
- **Caveats (my assessment):**
  - Small groups (about 21 each).
  - A low-level vigilance task, not complex knowledge work.
  - The "break" was a seconds-long secondary-task response, not rest.

**Kim, Park & Niu 2017 (online 2016), *Journal of Organizational Behavior* 38(1):28–44 (South Korea)**
- **Design:** 86 South Korean office workers, 10 consecutive workdays, 842 data points. Work demands were reported after lunch. Afternoon micro-break activities and end-of-day negative affect were reported later. — [MetaTOC abstract](https://metatoc.com/papers/51909-micro-break-activities-at-work-to-recover-from-daily-work-demands); [Univ. Vienna PDF citing it](https://ao-psy.univie.ac.at/fileadmin/user_upload/p_ao_psy/160271.pdf)
- **Results by activity:**
  - **Relaxation** (for example stretching or daydreaming) and **social** micro-breaks buffered the link between demands and negative affect.
  - **Nutrition** (snacks, coffee) had no significant effect.
  - **Cognitive** micro-breaks (for example reading news or email, browsing) **aggravated** the effect of demands on negative affect.
  — [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/51909-micro-break-activities-at-work-to-recover-from-daily-work-demands)
- **Caveat:** correlational diary design with self-report. Effect sizes were not retrieved.

**Henning, Jacques, Kissel, Sullivan & Alteras-Webb 1997, *Ergonomics* 40(1):78–91 (USA, University of Connecticut)**
- **Design:** computer operators at 2 field sites (n = 73 and n = 19) were prompted to take **three 30-s breaks plus one 3-min break every hour**, on top of their conventional breaks. Some also did stretching. — [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19236)
- **Compliance:** about **50% of the added breaks** were taken. Operators **preferred the 3-min breaks over the 30-s breaks**. — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19236)
- **Results:**
  - Larger site: no improvement in productivity or well-being.
  - Smaller site: productivity and eye, leg and foot comfort improved when the breaks included stretching.
  - Adding breaks, and so cutting work time, did not lower productivity.
  — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19236); [Wellnomics white paper (secondary, vendor)](https://wellnomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Wellnomics-white-paper-The-productivity-benefits-of-office-ergonomics-interventions.pdf)
- **Authors' conclusion:** breaks help "when the breaks integrate with task demands". — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19236)

**Galinsky, Swanson, Sauter, Hurrell & Schleifer 2000, *Ergonomics* (NIOSH, USA)**
- **Design:** 42 data-entry operators at an IRS centre, within-subjects, alternating schedules in 4-week blocks.
  - Conventional schedule: two 15-min breaks.
  - Supplementary schedule: the same plus **a 5-min break in each hour that had no break** (about 20 extra min per day).
  — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/198356); [NIOSH Update](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/niosh/updates/restbrks.html)
- **Results:** discomfort and eyestrain were lower under the supplementary schedule, and the rise in symptoms across the day was smaller. There was **no reduction in data-entry performance**: keystrokes per hour, documents per day and accuracy were unchanged according to a secondary summary. — [NIOSH Update](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/niosh/updates/restbrks.html); [Wellnomics (secondary)](https://wellnomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Wellnomics-white-paper-The-productivity-benefits-of-office-ergonomics-interventions.pdf)

**Galinsky et al. 2007, *American Journal of Industrial Medicine* 50(7):519–527 (NIOSH follow-up, USA)**
- **Design:** IRS data-entry operators (Cincinnati) did 4 weeks on conventional breaks (2 × 15 min/day) and 4 weeks on supplementary breaks (2 × 15 min plus 4 × 5 min/day). Half were randomised to stretch during breaks. 51 completed the symptom questionnaires (21 stretch, 30 no-stretch). — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/186286); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/18653)
- **Results:**
  - Supplementary breaks lowered discomfort and eyestrain.
  - **Data-entry speed was higher**, which offset the lost work time.
  - Stretching had **no detectable effect**. Compliance was low: stretches were done in only 25% of conventional and 39% of supplementary breaks.
  — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/186286); [Cochrane table (PMC6517177)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6517177/table/CD008570-tblf-0012)
- **Correction:** the 2007 paper is in *Am J Ind Med*, not *Occup Environ Med*.

**McLean, Tingley, Scott & Rickards 2001, *Applied Ergonomics* 32(3):225–237 (Canada)**
- **Design:** RCT with computer keying workers randomised to one of three microbreak protocols: **self-directed, every 20 min, or every 40 min**. Each worker was also compared against their own no-break sessions. Outcomes were EMG of the neck, low back, upper trapezius and wrist/finger extensors, perceived discomfort and productivity. — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19242); [ScholarHub](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/70f1b9bc-83ac-4b53-a0ab-f2eb4746b2df)
- **Result retrieved:** all microbreak protocols were associated with higher-frequency EMG cycling in the wrist extensors (p < 0.05), a marker of reduced muscle fatigue. — [search snippet / PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19242)
- I did not retrieve the abstract text on discomfort or productivity. As I recall it, the paper reported less discomfort with microbreaks (most consistently with the 20-min schedule) and no loss of productivity. **This is unverified in this session.**

**Other relevant primary studies**
- **Hunter & Wu 2016, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 101(2):302–311 (USA, Baylor):**
  - Design: experience sampling with 95 employees over 5 days, 959 break surveys.
  - Timing: breaks taken **earlier in the day (mid-morning)** restored more resources. The more hours had passed since the shift began, the fewer resources and the more symptoms followed a break.
  - Activity choice: **preferred or enjoyable** activities predicted fewer headaches, eyestrain and low-back pain.
  - No evidence that non-work activities were inherently better.
  — [APA Monitor 2019](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2019/01/break.aspx); [Baylor news](https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2015/new-research-confirms-how-take-better-workday-breaks)
- **Bennett, Gabriel & Calderwood 2020, *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 25(2):126–142 (USA):**
  - Design: 16 interviews with shift workers, then a randomised experiment with 232 undergraduates that crossed break duration with activity.
  - Result: "Some, but not all, micro-break conditions" brought energy and attention back to baseline. Condition-level details were not retrieved.
  — [Univ. of Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/examining-the-interplay-of-micro-break-durations-and-activities-f/)
- **Conlin, Hu & Barber 2021:** **40-s microbreaks** improved task performance and within-task recovery, but only through psychological detachment, not positive affect. "Mastery" breaks produced more detachment than relaxation breaks, with no difference in performance. — [RWU Docs](https://docs.rwu.edu/fcas_fp/953)

### Inferences
- The NIOSH design maps almost directly onto a 1-hour session: **one ~5-min break per hour** cut discomfort and eyestrain at no cost to output.
- Henning suggests that a denser schedule (3 × 30 s plus 1 × 3 min per hour) gets only about 50% compliance, and that users prefer fewer, longer breaks (around 3 min) to 30-s ones. That is a strong UX signal against nagging people with many tiny prompts.
- Stretching prompts have weak or unproven value, partly because compliance is low.

### Gaps
- McLean 2001 discomfort and productivity numbers were not verified from the primary source.
- Effect sizes for Ariga & Lleras and Kim et al. were not retrieved.

## 3. Movement micro-breaks and interrupting sitting (Dunstan, Bergouignan 2016, Wennberg 2016): effects on cognition and energy

### Takeaway
Short walking breaks during long sitting reliably raise **self-reported energy and vigor and reduce fatigue**, and they have clear metabolic benefits. **Cognitive benefits are not shown** in the controlled trials: cognition was null in both Bergouignan and Wennberg, and the systematic reviews call the evidence limited and mixed.

### Cited Findings
- **Bergouignan et al. 2016, *Int J Behav Nutr Phys Act* (USA, University of Colorado):**
  - Design: randomised crossover, 30 sedentary adults, 3 conditions.
    - SIT: 6 h of uninterrupted sitting.
    - ONE: one 30-min moderate treadmill walk in the morning.
    - MICRO: **six hourly 5-min moderate walks**.
  - Energy and vigor: both activity conditions improved them versus SIT.
  - **Only MICRO** improved mood, reduced fatigue and reduced food cravings at the end of the day versus SIT.
  - **No significant differences in cognition** between conditions.
  - All outcomes were self-reported (VAS, POMS).
  — [PubMed 27809874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27809874/); [PMC5094084](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/5094084)
  - Some lay coverage overstates the result, including a J&J article that also notes J&J support. — [J&J](https://jnj.com/health-and-wellness/health-benefits-of-5-minute-walk-breaks-at-work)
- **Wennberg et al. 2016, *BMJ Open* 6:e009630 (Australia/Sweden, Baker IDI):**
  - Design: pilot randomised crossover, 19 overweight or obese adults aged 45–75, comparing uninterrupted sitting with **3-min light walking every 30 min**.
  - Results: no significant differences in fatigue or cognition over time. Only heart rate was higher in the active condition.
  - Authors' conclusion: breaks "may be an effective fatigue countermeasure acutely". This is a cautious claim given the null fatigue result.
  — [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/2/e009630)
  - Correction: the paper is in BMJ Open, not Diabetes Care.
- **Dunstan et al. 2012, *Diabetes Care* 35:976–983 (Australia):** breaking up prolonged sitting reduced postprandial glucose and insulin. This is the metabolic rationale, not a cognitive one. — [Cited via BMJ Open/search](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/2/e009630)
- **Chueh et al. 2022, *BMJ Open* (Taiwan), systematic review:** 7 studies, 168 participants. "Limited evidence with mixed findings" on whether activity breaks during sitting improve cognition. — [BMJ Open 12(3):e050458](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/12/3/e050458); [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/acute-effect-of-breaking-up-prolonged-sitting-on-cognition-a-syst/)
- **medRxiv 2022 preprint meta-analysis:** 13 RCTs, 295 participants, 9 in the meta-analysis, on exercise breaks and cognition during sitting. **Not peer-reviewed. Authors and status were not verified.** — [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.29.22270085v1)
- **Chandrasekaran et al. 2021, *BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders* (India):** a mapping review, not a meta-analysis. It argues a cognitive benefit is "biologically plausible" (via glycaemia, inflammation and cerebral blood flow) but untested. — [BMC](https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12891-021-04136-5.pdf)

### Inferences
- For 1-hour sessions, a **~5-min movement break each hour** matches the Bergouignan MICRO protocol. Market it for energy, mood and fatigue, not for "smarter".
- Movement is the best-justified activity for the hourly break when the user sits all day, because it adds metabolic and musculoskeletal benefits on top of vigor.

### Gaps
- No well-powered RCT shows that sitting-break movement improves knowledge-work cognition. Samples are small (19–30) and largely sedentary or overweight adults.

## 4. Eye strain / computer vision syndrome and the 20-20-20 rule: is it evidence-based?

### Takeaway
The 20-20-20 rule is **widely recommended but thinly tested**. It means looking at something 20 ft (6 m) away for 20 s every 20 min. One small uncontrolled-in-time study (n = 29) found symptom relief that vanished 1 week after stopping. One lab study (n = 30) found no protective effect. Breaks in general (NIOSH) reduce eyestrain more convincingly.

### Cited Findings
- **Talens-Estarelles, Cerviño, García-Lázaro, Fogelton, Sheppard & Wolffsohn 2023, *Contact Lens & Anterior Eye* (Spain/UK/Slovakia; Valencia and Aston):**
  - Design: 29 symptomatic computer users (CVS-Q ≥ 6, at least 4 h a day of computer use) ran webcam software that detected gaze and natural breaks. **After 20 min of continuous viewing it prompted a 20-s look at a target ≥ 20 ft away.** Measurements were taken at baseline, after 2 weeks and 1 week after stopping.
  - Results:
    - Break behaviour changed: more breaks, and shorter work bouts (p ≤ 0.015).
    - **Digital eye strain and dry-eye symptoms decreased (p ≤ 0.045)**, but the improvement **was not maintained one week after discontinuation**.
    - No change in tear-film or ocular-surface signs.
    - No binocular-vision changes except accommodative facility (p = 0.010).
  - The introduction says only one prior study had examined the rule, with no evidence of compliance.
  - Limitation (my assessment): pre/post within-subject design with no parallel control group, so a placebo or expectancy effect is possible.
  — [Aston Research Explorer](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effects-of-breaks-on-digital-eye-strain-dry-eye-and-binocular/); [full text PDF](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/44152/1/1_s2.0_S1367048422001990_main.pdf)
- **The same paper on prevalence:** reported DES prevalence ranges from 33% to 65%, and is 74–77% among young adults. — [Aston PDF](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/44152/1/1_s2.0_S1367048422001990_main.pdf)
- **Johnson & Rosenfield (SUNY College of Optometry, USA):**
  - Design: 30 young subjects did a 40-min demanding tablet reading task on 4 occasions.
  - Result: symptoms rose in all conditions, and the authors found "no support for the 20-20-20 rule as an effective therapeutic prevention".
  - They also cautioned that this does not show breaks cannot help.
  - Details come via secondary trade press. The primary citation was not retrieved.
  — [Insight News](https://www.insightnews.com.au/does-the-20-20-20-rule-work-for-digital-eye-strain/); [Eyes On Eyecare Glance](https://glance.eyesoneyecare.com/stories/2023-01-25/20-20-20-rule-may-not-actually-help-digital-eye-strain/)
- **NIOSH supplementary-break studies (2000, 2007):** about 5-min breaks per hour reduced self-reported eyestrain in real data-entry work. See section 2. — [NIOSH Update](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/niosh/updates/restbrks.html); [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/186286)

### Inferences
- An app can include an optional 20-s "look far away" nudge about every 20 min as a **low-cost, low-evidence** feature. It should not be marketed as clinically proven. The benefit seems to depend on continued use.
- Within a 1-hour session, a 20-20-20 prompt at about minutes 20 and 40, plus a ~5-min break at 60, roughly approximates both the Talens-Estarelles and NIOSH protocols. Henning's compliance data suggest the 20-s prompts should be unobtrusive (for example a peripheral cue), not modal interruptions.

### Gaps
- No adequately powered RCT of the 20-20-20 rule with a parallel control group was found.
- The origin of the "20-20-20" numbers (commonly attributed to optometrist Jeffrey Anshel) was not verified with a source.

## 5. Which micro-break activities work best: relaxation, physical activity, nature viewing, social, or phone/social media?

### Takeaway
The best-supported options are **relaxation, social contact, movement and brief nature views**. **Cognitive or screen activities, and especially smartphone use, are the worst**: in one experiment, phone breaks produced as much depletion as taking no break. Personal preference and choice also matter.

### Cited Findings
- **Nature view (Lee, Williams, Sargent, Williams & Johnson 2015, *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 42:182–189, Australia, University of Melbourne):**
  - Design: 150 university students did a sustained-attention task (SART), then had a **40-s micro-break** viewing an image of a flowering-meadow **green roof** or a **bare concrete roof**, then repeated the task.
  - Results: the green-roof group made **fewer omission errors and responded more consistently**. The concrete group declined.
  — [U. Melbourne record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/977781-40-second-green-roof-views-sustain-attention--the-role-of-micro-breaks-in-attention-restoration); [Full text (ltl.org.uk PDF)](https://Www.ltl.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/40-second-green-roof-views-sustain-attention.pdf)
  - Caveat: the stimulus was an image on screen, a lab task and a student sample. The effect size was not retrieved.
- **Relaxation and social (Kim et al. 2017):** these buffered demands → negative affect. Cognitive micro-breaks aggravated it, and nutrition had no effect. — [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/51909-micro-break-activities-at-work-to-recover-from-daily-work-demands)
- **Phone breaks (Kang & Kurtzberg 2019, *Journal of Behavioral Addictions*, USA, Rutgers):**
  - Design: 414 participants solved anagrams. Halfway through, 3/4 of them took a break (a shopping task) on paper, on a computer or **on a cellphone**.
  - Results:
    - Phone-break participants took **19% longer** on the remaining task and solved **22% fewer** problems than the other break conditions combined.
    - "Cell phone breaks resulted in the same levels of cognitive depletion as not taking any break at all."
  — [Rutgers news](https://www.rutgers.edu/news/need-mental-break-avoid-your-cell-phone-rutgers-researchers-say); [PMC7044622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7044622)
  - Caveat: undergraduates and an anagram task.
- **Physical activity:**
  - Bergouignan 2016: hourly 5-min walks improved vigor, mood and fatigue. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27809874/)
  - Henning 1997: stretching breaks improved productivity and comfort at one of two sites. — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19236)
  - Galinsky 2007: no stretching effect, with low compliance. — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/186286)
- **Preference matters (Hunter & Wu 2016):** preferred activities predicted fewer symptoms, and earlier breaks were more restorative. — [APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2019/01/break.aspx)
- **Detachment:** 40-s microbreaks worked through psychological detachment. Mastery activities increased detachment more than relaxation did. — [Conlin et al. 2021, RWU](https://docs.rwu.edu/fcas_fp/953)
- **Meta-analytic level:** Albulescu 2022 could not compare cognitive, physical, relaxing and mixed activities because of heterogeneity. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)

### Inferences
- Suggested app content for breaks:
  1. Stand up and walk or move for a few minutes.
  2. Look out of a window or at greenery, or show a nature image for 40 s if there is no window.
  3. Breathing or relaxation.
  4. A quick chat with a colleague.
- Actively discourage phone, social media and news during the break. These are "cognitive" breaks that the evidence suggests fail to restore, or even harm.
- Let users pick from a menu, since choice and preference predict better recovery.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head RCT compares all activity types at matched durations in knowledge workers.
- Effect sizes for Lee 2015 and Kim 2017 were not retrieved.

## 6. Do micro-breaks disrupt flow or attention?

### Takeaway
No direct evidence shows that scheduled micro-breaks damage deep-work output. Meta-analytic performance effects are null-to-positive and never negative, and field studies show output is maintained despite less work time. Interruptions do carry resumption costs, though, and **imposed timing can land mid-thought**. The evidence on self-initiated versus imposed breaks is mixed. No study found measured "flow" disruption by micro-breaks specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **Albulescu 2022:** breaks did not worsen performance versus continuous work in any subgroup. Cognitive tasks gave d = -0.09, not significant. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)
- **Field studies:** NIOSH 2000/2007 and Henning 1997 found no productivity loss despite added break time. Galinsky 2007 found higher data-entry speed. — [NIOSH Update](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/niosh/updates/restbrks.html); [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/186286); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19236)
- **Ariga & Lleras 2011:** brief task switches **prevented** the attention decrement instead of disrupting performance. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/)
- **Katidioti et al. 2016, *Computers in Human Behavior* (Netherlands):**
  - Self-interruptions slowed main-task completion versus external interruptions at similar moments, because of about a 1-s decision cost seen in pupil data.
  - **Resumption lag did not differ** between the two kinds of interruption.
  — [interruptions.net PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Katidioti-ComputHumBehav16.pdf)
- **A requirements-engineering field study** found the opposite pattern: self-interruptions caused more task fragments and longer resumption lags. — [arXiv 1707.00794](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.00794)
- **Henning 1997:** only about 50% compliance with prompted breaks. Workers skip breaks that don't fit the task flow, and the authors stress that breaks should "integrate with task demands". — [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/19236)
- **Kang & Kurtzberg 2019:** authors recommend leaving extra transition time after a break before returning to demanding tasks, especially after phone use. — [Rutgers](https://www.rutgers.edu/news/need-mental-break-avoid-your-cell-phone-rutgers-researchers-say)

### Inferences
- Design implications for a 1-hour session app:
  - (a) Default to **one ~5-min break at the end of each ~60-min session**. This is the best evidence-backed unit (NIOSH, Bergouignan).
  - (b) Make any **intra-session micro-pauses** optional and **flexible in timing**. For example, offer a 20–40-s "look away" nudge around minute 20 to 30 that the user can snooze or defer to a natural stopping point, instead of hard interruptions.
  - (c) Avoid many tiny forced prompts, given Henning's 50% compliance and preference for 3-min breaks.
  - (d) Encourage breaks earlier in the day (Hunter & Wu).
- Because Albulescu found no gain for cognitively demanding tasks, deep-work users should get well-being, not productivity, as the value proposition for mid-session pauses.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring subjective flow (for example with the Flow Short Scale) before and after imposed micro-breaks.
- The 2008 *Creativity Research Journal* study comparing imposed and self-timed breaks was found, but its results were not retrieved. — [UvA DARE](https://dare.uva.nl/id/0946fb1d-4875-413b-8c6c-8cc3f44b90da)
- No study directly compares a single 5-min break per hour with several 30-s micro-pauses on knowledge-work quality.
