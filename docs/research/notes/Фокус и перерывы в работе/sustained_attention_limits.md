# Natural Limits of Sustained Attention on Cognitively Demanding Work (vigilance decrement, mental fatigue, ultradian rhythms, expertise, attention-span myths, individual differences)

> Scope note: the notes were compiled Oct 2026 from web search, abstracts, and two full-text PDFs (Helton & Russell 2015; Bradbury 2016). Statements marked **[abstract/secondary only]** were not checked against the paper's full text. Statements marked **[WEAK/POPULAR]** are claims that circulate widely but lack solid peer-reviewed support.

## 1. Vigilance / sustained-attention research: when does performance start to decline, and why?

### Takeaway
The vigilance decrement is real and robust. In classic low-event, monotonous monitoring tasks it is clearly present within the first ~30 minutes (Mackworth 1948). In demanding, high-event-rate tasks it can appear within **under 5 minutes**. So there is no single universal "attention clock": the time to decline depends on task demand, event rate, memory load and motivation. A pooled effect for the sensitivity decline is moderate (d ≈ 0.5–0.7). Breaks reliably restore performance, and real rest works better than switching to another demanding task.

### Cited Findings
**Classic origin and onset timing**
- Mackworth (1948, UK; RAF radar-monitoring "clock test"): the decrement was robust **after the first half hour on task** — [Frontiers in Cognition review "The vigilance decrement: its first 75 years" (2025, Canadian authors)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full). (Original: Mackworth NH, *QJEP* 1:6–21, 1948, cited in [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).)
- McCormack (1958): reaction times increased over a **40–50 min** vigil. An interruption after 40 min improved RTs, and longer breaks helped more — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).
- Jenkins (1958): **30-second interruptions every 5 min** markedly reduced both the detection decline and the RT increase — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).
- J. F. Mackworth (1961 monograph): a salient interruption (a phone call or a half-hour break) or performance feedback reduced or eliminated the decrement. Merely urging people to be attentive had **no effect** — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).
- Classic vigilance tasks typically last **40 min to several hours**. The decrement is defined as a falling probability of detecting rare critical signals with time on task, plus slower and more variable RTs — [PMC6109784, "Examining the Role of Task Requirements in the Magnitude of the Vigilance Decrement" (2018)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6109784/).
- Demanding tasks produce a decrement in **less than 5 minutes**. Helton & Russell (2015, Univ. of Canterbury, New Zealand) write that "if the task demand is intense enough, the vigilance decrement can occur extremely rapidly". Their visuospatial task (blocks of 52.8 s / 1.76 min) shows this — [Helton & Russell 2015, *Cognition* 134:165–173](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).
- Neural time-on-task effects in the vigilance meta-analysis by Langner & Eickhoff (2013, Germany; *Psychological Bulletin* 139:870–900) were reported over task durations from **14 to 1800 s** (0.25–30 min). Decrements were larger when tasks were simple and repetitive — [Langner & Eickhoff meta-analysis](https://www.academia.edu/15499833/Sustaining_attention_to_simple_tasks_A_meta_analytic_review_of_the_neural_mechanisms_of_vigilant_attention) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- The magnitude of the decrement depends on task factors: event rate, memory load, and modality — [PMC6109784 (2018)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6109784/); [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full). Parasuraman (1979, *Science*, US): sensitivity declined mainly in successive-discrimination, high-event-rate tasks — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).

**Effect sizes (meta-analysis)**
- See, Howe, Warm & Dember (1995, US, *Psychological Bulletin*) pooled **42 studies / 138 conditions** (English-language, 1980–1992, adults). Effect size = (first-period minus last-period sensitivity) / √MSE. The **overall mean was 0.71** in Table 1, but the Figure 6 summary gives a **mean of 0.73 and a median of 0.55** (an internal inconsistency). By task type: simultaneous/high-rate/sensory 0.74 (30 conditions); simultaneous/high-rate/cognitive 0.47 (30); successive/high-rate/sensory 0.72 (45) — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full); [See et al. 1995 on ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232476815_Meta-analysis_of_the_sensitivity_decrement_in_vigilance).
- Swets (1977, US): a sensitivity decrement "cannot be taken for granted". When it was significant, it was about 20% — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).
- Modern measured declines are often small in absolute terms. Feltmate et al. (2020, Canada; ANTI-Vea task) found A′ ≈ 0.905 → 0.886; Luna et al. (2018, Spain) found ≈ 0.926 → 0.905 — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).

**Rest vs. task-switching (key for break design) — Helton & Russell 2015, NZ, full text read**
- Exp. 1: **N = 266** students. Rest of about 1.76 min with a countdown vs. a different vigilance task vs. continuing the task. Sensitivity (A′) **improved after rest** (0.915 → 0.931, p = .040). It declined after letter-task "breaks" (0.910 → 0.886) and after continuous work (0.908 → 0.883). The RT increase was +18 ms after rest, +33 ms after the letter task, and **+70 ms** with continuous work — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).
- Exp. 2: **N = 521**, five interruption types. Within about 3.5 min of work before the break, A′ fell from .900 to .865 (ηp² = .10) and RT rose from 505 to 554 ms (ηp² = .41). After the interruption, A′ changes were: **rest +0.053** (significant); verbal memory task +0.024 (n.s.); letter-detection task +0.004; spatial memory task −0.011; **continuous −0.043** (significant). The rank order matched the resource-theory prediction (p = .017). The authors conclude "a complete rest is best", and that breaks filled with tasks sharing the same resources (e.g., spatial) give less recovery — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).
- Rest breaks may fail if the person does not know when the break ends, because the break then becomes "a vigilance task" itself (Lim et al. 2013, cited) — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).

**Theories: resource depletion vs. underload / mind-wandering vs. resource-control**
- **Resource (overload) theory**, from Warm, Parasuraman & Matthews (2008, US, *Human Factors* 50:433–441, "Vigilance requires hard mental work and is stressful") and Davies & Parasuraman (1982), is "arguably the predominate view held by the majority of active vigilance researchers". The decrement tracks declining cerebral blood flow in the ACC and prefrontal cortex with time on task (Hitchcock 2003; Shaw 2009) — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).
- **Underload / mindlessness / mind-wandering** accounts (Robertson et al. 1997 SART, UK; Manly et al. 1999; Cunningham et al. 2000) and the **goal-habituation** account (Ariga & Lleras 2011, Univ. of Illinois, US) attribute the decrement to disengagement from a monotonous task, not to depletion — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full); [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).
- Ariga & Lleras (2011, *Cognition* 118:439–443): the decrement was averted when participants were sporadically asked to recall memorized digits during the vigil (brief goal switches). Press materials describe **84 participants in four groups and a ~50-min task**, but another summary says 40 min, so the exact duration is unconfirmed — [PubMed 21211793](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/); [Univ. of Illinois news](https://news.illinois.edu/brief-diversions-vastly-improve-focus-researchers-find/).
- Failed replication: Helton & Russell (2012, *Exp Brain Res* 219:37–46), "Brief mental breaks and content-free cues may not keep you focused", reported substantial evidence for the null — [Springer Medicine summary](https://www.springermedicine.com/brief-mental-breaks-and-content-free-cues-may-not-keep-you-focus/25692108).
- **Resource-control account**: Thomson, Besner & Smilek (2015, Univ. of Waterloo, Canada; *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10:82–96). Neither theory alone explains the data. A controller allocates resources to the task, and that allocation drifts toward mind-wandering (the "default") over time, so executive control is needed to keep the task goal active — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691614556681/). The account was contested: a critique (2016–17, UCF/Hancock group) argued that Thomson et al. misinterpreted prior vigilance research on sensitivity vs. bias — [UCF STARS record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/7784).
- **Opportunity-cost model**: Kurzban, Duckworth, Kable & Myers (2013, US, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 36:661–679) treat effort and fatigue as a signal of the opportunity cost of continuing a task rather than literal depletion (cited in [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf), who argue against equating resource theory with ego depletion).
- Methodological caveat: apparent sensitivity declines can be artifacts of response-bias shifts when false-alarm rates are at floor (Thomson et al. 2016; McCarley et al. 2021 simulations) — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).
- Cross-species benchmark: explosive-detection dogs could search effectively for only **45–60 min** before needing rest (Helton 2009) — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full).

### Inferences
- Vigilance studies use monotonous signal-detection tasks, not the engaging, self-paced knowledge work a focus app targets. They set a lower bound (decline within 5–30 min for boring or very intense monitoring), not a ceiling for meaningful creative or analytic work.
- Both main theory families predict the same practical rule: performance degrades with continuous time on one task, and real breaks (resource view) or goal resets (disengagement view) restore it. A ~60-min session with a real break is consistent with the decrement literature. Brief self-paced micro-pauses inside the session are also supported (Jenkins 1958; Helton & Russell 2015).
- The 2015 NZ data suggest the break should be genuine rest. Switching to another screen task (e.g., checking messages) gives less recovery, especially if it uses the same cognitive modality.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Mackworth's (1948) exact numbers (commonly quoted as a ~10–15% drop in detections in the first 30 min of a 2-h watch). That figure comes from memory and secondary teaching sources and is not verified here.
- The full text of See et al. (1995) and of Langner & Eickhoff (2013) was not accessed. Minute-by-minute decline curves were not obtained.
- Warm, Parasuraman & Matthews (2008) NASA-TLX workload values were not retrieved. The paper is commonly summarized as showing that vigilance workload is high, but no numbers were verified here.
- There is no vigilance study of realistic self-paced knowledge work (e.g., programming or writing) with objective performance measured continuously over 60–120 min.

## 2. Mental fatigue research: after how long does time-on-task fatigue measurably impair performance?

### Takeaway
Lab mental-fatigue research typically induces fatigue with **≥30 min to 3 h** of continuous demanding cognitive work. By convention, about 30 min is treated as the minimum for a "fatiguing" manipulation. Continuous work of 2–3 h without rest reliably produces subjective fatigue, slower RTs, more errors, and EEG changes. Effects on later physical performance are real but small. There is no precise, universal "minute X" at which fatigue begins.

### Cited Findings
- **Boksem, Meijman & Lorist (2005, Univ. of Groningen, Netherlands; *Cognitive Brain Research* 25:107–116)**: participants did a visual attention task for **3 h without rest**. Subjective fatigue and theta and lower-alpha EEG power rose. **RTs, misses and false alarms increased with time on task**. N1 and N2b ERP components changed with time on task, which the authors read as reduced goal-directed (top-down) attention as fatigue grew — [Boksem et al. 2005 PDF (author site)](https://www.boksem.nl/pdf/boksem2005cbr.pdf) **[abstract/secondary only; PDF link now returns 404]**.
- **Lorist, Boksem & Ridderinkhof (2005, Netherlands; *Cognitive Brain Research*)**: **2 h** of continuous demanding task performance. Fatigue was linked to changes in error-related brain activity (anterior cingulate / performance monitoring) — [Lorist et al. 2005 PDF](https://www.boksem.nl/pdf/lorist2005cbr.pdf) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- **Van Cutsem, Marcora, De Pauw, Bailey, Meeusen & Roelands (2017, Vrije Universiteit Brussel, Belgium, with Univ. of Kent, UK; *Sports Medicine* 47(8):1569–1588)**: systematic review of 11 studies (6 strong, 5 moderate quality; PubMed and Web of Science to April 2016). It **excluded studies with mental tasks shorter than 30 min**, so ≥30 min of demanding cognitive activity is the working definition of a fatigue-inducing dose. Mental fatigue **impaired endurance performance** without changing heart rate, lactate, VO2 or cardiac output. Maximal strength and power were **not affected** — [JCU repository record](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/50619/).
- **Habay, Van Cutsem et al. (meta-regression, Belgium, "The individual response to mental fatigue")**: a small but significant effect of mental fatigue on endurance performance, with large individual variation — [Royal Military Academy (Belgium) research portal](https://researchportal.rma.ac.be/en/publications/the-individual-response-to-mental-fatigue-a-systematic-review-and/) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- Micro-breaks meta-analysis, **Albulescu et al. (2022, Romania; *PLOS ONE*)**: 19 records, 22 samples, **N = 2,335**. Micro-breaks (≤10 min) increased vigor (**d = 0.36**, k = 9, n = 913) and reduced fatigue (**d = 0.35**, k = 9, n = 803). The overall performance effect was **not significant (d = 0.16, p = .116)**, with benefits only for less cognitively demanding tasks. Longer breaks gave larger performance gains, and the authors suggest that recovery from highly depleting tasks may need **breaks longer than 10 min** — [DOAJ record of Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f).

### Inferences
- The fatigue literature does not define a sharp threshold at 60 min. Typical protocols show reliable fatigue at 30–180 min of uninterrupted demanding work. A 60-min block with a meaningful break sits comfortably inside the range where fatigue is beginning but not severe.
- For cognitively heavy tasks, Albulescu et al. suggest that 5-min micro-breaks restore subjective energy but not necessarily performance. After a ~60-min deep-work block, a **10–20 min** break may be more defensible than a 5-min one. This is an inference, not a tested prescription.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain the exact minute at which RTs or misses first became significantly worse in Boksem 2005 (e.g., whether the change was already visible in the first 20–30 min block).
- Lorist et al. (2000, *Psychophysiology*, "Mental fatigue and task control: planning and preparation") was identified but not read. Its details (duration, N) are unverified.
- I found no study that directly compares 25-, 50-, 60- and 90-min work blocks on objective output in real knowledge work.

## 3. Is there credible evidence for 90-minute ultradian cycles (Kleitman's BRAC) in waking cognitive performance?

### Takeaway
**Weak / largely popular claim.** Kleitman's Basic Rest-Activity Cycle (BRAC, proposed in the 1960s) holds that the ~90-min NREM–REM sleep cycle continues through the day. There is some support in **sleep propensity** data: Lavie's ultrashort sleep-wake schedules show ~90–100-min "sleepability" rhythms. But a direct test of waking cognitive performance (60 subjects, every 10 min for 9 h) found **no significant 90-min periodicity**. Reviews state that a common BRAC linking night-time sleep cycles and waking performance has not been confirmed. Productivity blogs overstate this evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Lavie & Scherson (1981, Technion, Israel; *Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology*), "Ultrashort sleep-waking schedule. I. Evidence of ultradian rhythmicity in 'sleepability'". Results were interpreted within Kleitman's BRAC model and showed ultradian rhythmicity in the ability to fall asleep. Note this is a **sleepiness** measure, not cognitive performance — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0013469481901644) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- "Ultradian rhythms in cognitive performance: no evidence for a 1.5-h rhythm" (*Biological Psychology*, 1995): **60 subjects** were tested **every 10 min for 9 h** on a sentence-verification task plus alertness and mood ratings. Spectral analysis found **no significant 90-min periodicity** for any variable — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/030105119505121P) **[abstract/secondary only; author names not confirmed in this search]**.
- A review chapter on ultradian cognitive rhythms during sleep deprivation notes that a common BRAC linking the nightly ~90-min NREM–REM cycle to waking alertness and performance rhythms **has not been confirmed**. Some studies instead found a slower ~4-h rhythm — [Springer chapter "Ultradian Cognitive Performance Rhythms During Sleep Deprivation"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4020-8352-5_13) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- A 1995 study of task performance, self-evaluation and EEG found a rhythm of about **12 cycles/day** (~2 h) in behavior and ratings, and slower (~6/day) and faster (10–18/day) EEG components. The authors read this as supporting a **multi-oscillator** model rather than a single 90-min clock — [BISp-SURF record "Ultradian rhythms in task performance, self-evaluation, and EEG activity"](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU199501075076) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- **[WEAK/POPULAR]** Productivity sites present "90-minute focus cycles" as established neuroscience. For example, they claim Lavie's group documented the cycles across performance measures, which the peer-reviewed abstracts do not support — e.g., [Exoplan blog](https://exoplan.io/blog/ultradian-rhythms/), [Neurosity guide](https://neurosity.co/guides/ultradian-rhythm-90-minute-brain-cycles), [goalsandprogress.com](https://goalsandprogress.com/ultradian-rhythm-work-schedule/). The popular "90-min" idea is largely traced to Ernest Rossi's and Tony Schwartz's popular books (*see Gaps*).

### Inferences
- An app should **not** claim that 60 or 90 minutes is "aligned with your ultradian rhythm" as established science. At best, ultradian periodicity in arousal or sleepiness is plausible but inconsistent, with periods ranging from ~90 min to ~4 h and large individual variation.
- A 60-min default is no less evidence-based than 90 min. Neither duration is directly supported by a demonstrated waking cognitive cycle.

### Gaps
- Primary Kleitman (1963, *Sleep and Wakefulness*; 1982 "Basic rest-activity cycle — 22 years later", *Sleep*) and Rossi (*The 20-Minute Break*, 1991) texts were not retrieved, so the claimed origin of the popular 90-min productivity meme is from general knowledge, not verified.
- The author names and full results of the 1995 "no evidence for a 1.5-h rhythm" paper were not confirmed. It is likely Neubauer & Freudenthaler (Graz, Austria), but this is unverified.
- Lavie's later "sleep gates" work (1986) was not retrieved directly.

## 4. Expertise studies: daily capacity for focused work (Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993)

### Takeaway
Ericsson et al. (1993, Max Planck Institute for Human Development, Berlin / Florida State; *Psychological Review* 100:363–406) argue that deliberate practice is effortful and limited. They report essentially **no benefit beyond ~4 h/day**, reduced benefit beyond ~2 h/day, and an effective dose **near 1 h/day** for some perceptual-motor skills. Critics note that the underlying training studies are very small, so the numbers are rough. The popular "4 hours of deep work a day" rule (Cal Newport) is a loose reading of this paper.

### Cited Findings
- The paper states that training studies show essentially no benefit from practice exceeding **4 h per day** and reduced benefits from practice exceeding **2 h**. For typing and other perceptual-motor skills, the effective duration of deliberate practice "may be closer to **1 hr per day**". Beginners, especially children, start at about **an hour or less per day** — [Epistemic Spot Check (acesounderglass, 2019)](https://acesounderglass.com/2019/06/21/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the-acquisition-of-expert-performance/); [jsomers.net](https://jsomers.net/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/326); [Andy Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z6xWa5QDyUmvuDqZ9Z7sANrfjf8sLoJ7BnspW).
- The paper's conclusion, per the spot-checker, puts the practical ceiling at about **3.5 h** of deliberate practice. The cited typing study (Baddeley & Longman) found learning most efficient with **one 1-hour session per day** — [acesounderglass](https://acesounderglass.com/2019/06/21/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the-acquisition-of-expert-performance/).
- Critique: supporting training studies were "criminally small" (e.g., Schneider & Shiffrin n = 8; Seibel n = 3). The blogger concludes "the precision of these studies is greatly exaggerated" and sees "no reason to believe" the 3.5-h figure — [acesounderglass](https://acesounderglass.com/2019/06/21/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the-acquisition-of-expert-performance/) (blog, not peer-reviewed).
- The "4 hours a day" answer usually quoted for thought work comes via Cal Newport's *Deep Work*, which uses Ericsson as its source — [acesounderglass](https://acesounderglass.com/2019/06/21/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the-acquisition-of-expert-performance/).
- A 1995 McMaster thesis testing deliberate-practice theory in wrestlers found that practice-alone activities did not discriminate skill groups — [MacSphere](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/handle/11375/19414).

### Inferences
- If ~1 h is an effective single-session unit and 3–4 h is a daily ceiling for the most demanding focused work, then **3–4 × ~60-min sessions per day** fits the expertise literature well. An app could reasonably suggest a daily cap of about 4 deep sessions rather than unlimited sessions.

### Gaps
- I could not verify from the primary text the frequently repeated details about the Berlin violinists: that sessions were **limited to about 60–90 min**, that elite violinists took afternoon **naps**, and that the study used **10 "best" vs. 10 "good" vs. 10 "teacher"** students (n = 30). These are widely cited in secondary literature but were not confirmed in this search.
- Later meta-analyses (Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014, *Psychological Science*), which found that deliberate practice explains only part of the variance in expertise, were not retrieved here.

## 5. Is the "8-second / goldfish attention span" claim debunked? What is the real picture?

### Takeaway
**Yes, debunked / [WEAK/POPULAR].** The "8 seconds, shorter than a goldfish's 9" figure came from a 2015 Microsoft Canada consumer-insights report. That report attributed the number to a non-academic aggregator (Statistic Brain), and no underlying study was found. The number reflects time spent on a web page, not a capacity for sustained attention. The related "10–15-minute lecture attention span" also rests on weak, misread evidence (Bradbury 2016). Attention researchers describe basic attentional capacity as stable over decades.

### Cited Findings
- Bradbury (2016, Rosalind Franklin Univ., Chicago, US; *Advances in Physiology Education* 40:509–513, "Personal View", full text read) — [PubMed 28145268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/); [full-text PDF](https://epale.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/advan.00109.2016.pdf):
  - The 8-s figure was reported in *Time* (2015) from a Microsoft-commissioned study. "No one knows how to actually measure a goldfish's attention span". What was measured in goldfish is memory, which is "actually quite good" (Univ. of Plymouth; Gee et al. 1994). The human 8 s "actually reflects the average time a person will spend on a web page before looking somewhere else".
  - The 10–15-min lecture claim traces back to Hartley & Davies (1978), a **review of note-taking**, not attention. The decline in note-taking occurred in the **last** 10 min and reflected less lecture content, not fatigue.
  - Stuart & Rutherford (1978, *Lancet*, UK medical students): self-rated attention every 5 min **rose during the first 10–20 min, then declined slowly** through a 50-min lecture, never falling below the starting level. Differences were driven by the lecturer.
  - Johnstone & Percival (1976): observed "attention breaks" at 0–5 and 10–18 min, but with undefined criteria, and 87% of the data came from a single observer.
  - Bunce et al. (2010, US chemistry classes, clickers): attention waxed and waned throughout, with no single cliff.
  - McLeish (1968): no difference in retention between live lectures of 25, 40, or 50 min.
  - Conclusion: "the available primary data do not support the concept of a 10- to 15-min attention limit". The largest variability comes from **differences between teachers**.
- The Microsoft report (2015) said attention fell from 12 s (2000) to 8 s and compared this to a goldfish's 9 s. The statistic was attributed to Statistic Brain, and a BBC reporter found **no research** behind it. What the Microsoft research itself studied was multi-screening and social media habits — [WARC "The goldfish myth"](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/opinion/the_goldfish_myth/2806); [UX Psychology "The attention span myth"](https://uxpsychology.substack.com/p/the-attention-span-myth); [LinkedIn Marketing blog (2016)](https://business.linkedin.com/marketing-solutions/blog/best-practices--content-marketing/2016/the-great-goldfish-attention-span-myth--and-why-its-killing-cont). Some commentators dispute whether the report itself even contained the 8-s/goldfish figure, so its exact provenance remains murky.
- Michael Posner (Univ. of Oregon) is quoted saying there is no real evidence that attention span has changed since it was first reported in the late 1800s. Other researchers call it "remarkably stable across decades" — [UX Psychology](https://uxpsychology.substack.com/p/the-attention-span-myth) (secondary quotes).
- A 2022 King's College London survey found that about half of UK adults still believed the 8-second claim — [Medium "The attention span stat everyone keeps getting wrong"](https://medium.com/write-rise/the-attention-span-stat-everyone-keeps-getting-wrong-91c418793ccc) (secondary).

### Inferences
- "Attention span" is not a single measurable trait in seconds. Sustained attention is task- and motivation-dependent (cf. Section 1). The app should avoid repeating the 8-s or 10-min claims in marketing.

### Gaps
- The Bradbury PDF does not trace who originally created the 8-s number. The Statistic Brain attribution comes from journalistic and blog sources, not peer review.
- Gloria Mark's (UC Irvine) field data on average on-screen attention shifts (~47 s in recent years) were not retrieved. That figure measures switching behavior, not capacity, and would need checking before use.

## 6. Individual differences: chronotype, time of day, age, task type

### Takeaway
Limits vary substantially. **Task type and demand** are the biggest modulators: simple, monotonous, high-event-rate, memory-loaded tasks decline fastest, sometimes within minutes. Engaging, varied, self-paced tasks decline slower. **Chronotype/time-of-day** effects (the "synchrony effect") appear in roughly half of studies of young adults, mostly on attention and inhibition, and more consistently in older adults. **Age** does not straightforwardly worsen the vigilance decrement, and older adults sometimes sustain attention *better* (often attributed to motivation and less mind-wandering). **Motivation, feedback and lecturer/task quality** strongly modulate attention.

### Cited Findings
- **Task type**: the decrement is larger with simple, repetitive tasks (Langner & Eickhoff 2013) and depends on event rate, memory load and modality (Parasuraman 1979; See et al. 1995 effect sizes 0.47–0.74 by task class) — [Langner & Eickhoff](https://www.academia.edu/15499833/Sustaining_attention_to_simple_tasks_A_meta_analytic_review_of_the_neural_mechanisms_of_vigilant_attention); [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full); [PMC6109784](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6109784/). Concurrent working-memory load worsens the decrement (Helton & Russell 2011, 2013; Caggiano & Parasuraman 2004) — [Helton & Russell 2015](https://interruptions.net/literature/Helton-Cognition15.pdf).
- **Chronotype / synchrony effect** — Chauhan et al. (2025, UK — Kingston/Brunel; *Chronobiology International* 42(4)), systematic review of **65 studies**: most found **no main effect of chronotype** on cognition. About **45% of studies in adults aged 18–45** showed a synchrony effect (better performance at the preferred time of day), mostly for attention, inhibition and memory. **Most older-adult studies** showed it. Task complexity and lighting moderate the effect — [Kingston University record](https://researchinnovation.kingston.ac.uk/en/publications/chronotype-and-synchrony-effects-in-human-cognitive-performance-a/); [Brunel BURA](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/31067).
- Schmidt et al. (2026, Univ. of Liège, Belgium; *Journal of Sleep Research*), extreme morning vs. evening types on an auditory oddball attention task: **no overall behavioral main or interaction effects**. RTs slowed late in the evening session for morning types only. Inferior frontal cortex activation was higher at each type's optimal time (stronger top-down control) — [ORBi Liège](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/347408).
- A 2023 review (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*) describes the synchrony effect as most pronounced for **strong chronotypes** and for **effortful or distractor-suppression** tasks — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/17456916231178553/) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- **Age**: Robison et al. (2022, US; *Psychology and Aging*), multimodal study: older adults sustained attention **better** than younger adults and showed **no vigilance decrement** — [Robison et al. 2022 PDF](https://sites.wustl.edu/ccalab/files/2022/08/robison_etal_2022.pdf). A meta-analysis by Vallesi, Tronelli, Lomi & Pezzetta (2021, Italy) reports that older adults sometimes outperform younger adults on sustained-attention measures (cited in Robison et al.) — [Robison et al. 2022 PDF](https://sites.wustl.edu/ccalab/files/2022/08/robison_etal_2022.pdf). Earlier literature is mixed: in one review, 6 of 11 studies showed a decrement and only 4 showed older adults underperforming — [Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnagi.2017.00021/pdf). A 1989 digit-discrimination study found greater decrement in older adults with degraded stimuli — [PubMed 2803627](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2803627). A 2017 paper notes "no consensus" on aging effects — [Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnagi.2017.00021/pdf).
- **Mental fatigue susceptibility varies widely between individuals**, per the Belgian meta-regression "The individual response to mental fatigue" — [RMA research portal](https://researchportal.rma.ac.be/en/publications/the-individual-response-to-mental-fatigue-a-systematic-review-and/) **[abstract/secondary only]**.
- **Motivation/context**: feedback and salient interruptions eliminate decrements, while exhortation does not (Mackworth 1961) — [Frontiers 2025 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1632885/full). In lectures, differences between teachers outweigh time effects — [Bradbury 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/).

### Inferences
- A fixed 60-min session is a reasonable **default**, but the evidence supports **user-adjustable** durations (e.g., 25–90 min) with adaptive suggestions. For example, shorter sessions for monotonous or high-load tasks or off-peak hours, and longer ones for engaging flow tasks.
- Scheduling hard sessions at the user's chronotype-preferred time has modest but real support, especially for attention and inhibition tasks and for strong morning or evening types.

### Gaps
- No study found tests how chronotype changes the *time-to-decline* within a session, as opposed to overall level.
- Sleep deprivation, caffeine, and ADHD as modulators of sustained attention were outside the retrieved sources (PVT and sleep-loss literature such as Dinges, Lim & Dinges 2010 were not fetched).
- Gender and cultural differences were not examined.
