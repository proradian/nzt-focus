# Work/Break Schedules: Pomodoro, Fixed vs Self-Regulated Breaks, Longer Recovery Breaks, and Daily Focus Capacity

Scope note: notes for designing a focus app built around ~1-hour work sessions. Peer-reviewed sources are marked [PR]; vendor and pop-science sources are marked [NON-PR]. Every claim has an inline source. Where a figure comes only from secondary coverage, that is stated.

---

## Q1. Empirical tests of Pomodoro and of fixed-interval vs self-regulated breaks

### Takeaway
There is no well-powered RCT showing that Pomodoro (25/5) improves output. Three small Maastricht University (Netherlands) experiments with students (N = 87, 94, 176) found **no difference in task completion, productivity, flow or learning** between fixed and self-regulated breaks. Effects on mood and fatigue point in **opposite directions** across the studies: Biwer 2023 favoured fixed breaks, while Smits 2025 and Göksu 2026 favoured self-regulated breaks. Note: **Smits et al. 2025 is an experiment, not a systematic review.**

### Cited Findings

**Biwer, de Bruin & Persky (2023), *British Journal of Educational Psychology* 93(2):353–367, DOI 10.1111/bjep.12593 [PR, Netherlands]**
- Sample and design: 87 Dutch bachelor's and master's students self-studied for one day. There were three conditions:
  - self-regulated breaks (n = 35)
  - "systematic-long / Pomodoro": a 6-min break after every 24-min block (n = 25)
  - "systematic-short": a 3-min break after every 12-min block (n = 27)
  — [PubMed 36859717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/); [Maastricht CRIS record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel)
- Results:
  - Self-regulating students took longer study sessions and longer breaks.
  - That pattern was linked to higher fatigue and distractedness, and to lower concentration and motivation.
  - Mental effort and task completion did not differ between groups.
  - The authors concluded that systematic breaks had mood benefits and apparent efficiency benefits, meaning similar task completion in less time.
  — [PubMed 36859717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)
- Caveat: secondary sources misreport this study. Blogs describe the blocks as 25 minutes (the paper used 24), and one citation gives the wrong volume and pages (93(3):1096–1113). The authors themselves say the effort spent regulating breaks needs more research. — [PubMed 36859717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)

**Smits, Wenzel & de Bruin (2025), "Investigating the Effectiveness of Self-Regulated, Pomodoro, and Flowtime Break-Taking Techniques Among Students," *Behavioral Sciences* 15(7):861, published 25 June 2025 [PR, MDPI; Netherlands, Maastricht]**
- Design: 94 students, one 2-hour self-study session, three conditions:
  - self-regulated breaks
  - Pomodoro (a 5-min break after every 25 min)
  - Flowtime (the student breaks when focus fades)
  — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Investigating-the-Effectiveness-of-Self-Regulated,-Smits-Wenzel/defa071a4ae9da869677deb1df8d9c0d503cf7f3); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393287549_Investigating_the_Effectiveness_of_Self-Regulated_Pomodoro_and_Flowtime_Break-Taking_Techniques_Among_Students)
- Overall result: across the whole session there were no differences in fatigue, motivation, productivity, task completion or flow. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/7/861) (abstract, as relayed in search results; the full text was 403-blocked)
- Trajectories:
  - Pomodoro breaks produced a **faster increase in fatigue** than self-regulated breaks.
  - Pomodoro and Flowtime breaks both produced a **faster decrease in motivation** than self-regulated breaks.
  — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/7/861)
- Details from a blog summary [NON-PR, secondary; check against the paper]:
  - group sizes: self-regulated n = 25, Pomodoro n = 36, Flowtime n = 33
  - Flowtime break rule: 5 min after ≤25 min of work, 8 min after 25–50 min, 10 min after ≥50 min
  - about 70–80% of planned tasks were completed in every group
  - flow scored about 4 on a 0–7 scale in every group
  — [yattask blog](https://flow.yattask.app/blog/en/pomodoro-flowtime-self-regulated-study)
- Preprint discrepancy: the March 2025 preprint said Flowtime led to a *slower* increase in fatigue. The published version does not report this, so cite the published version. — [preprints.org 202503.0845](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202503.0845/v1); [Sciety](https://labs.sciety.org/articles/by?article_doi=10.20944%2Fpreprints202503.0845.v1)

**Göksu, Wiradhany & de Bruin (2026), "When to Take a Break: Comparing Effects of Systematic Short (Pomodoro) and Self-Regulated Breaks on Subjective Experience and Actual Learning," *Behavioral Sciences* 16(7):1158, published 9 July 2026 [PR, preregistered randomized online experiment; Netherlands/Indonesia]**
- Sample: 176 students analysed (90 systematic, 86 self-regulated), from 460 registrants. 76% were female and the mean age was about 21.6. — [PMC13405840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/)
- Conditions: one difficult reading text with a 60-min cap.
  - Systematic: up to four 12-min reading blocks, each followed by an automatic 3-min break.
  - Self-regulated: the student started breaks with a button.
  — [PMC13405840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/)
- Results:
  - Comprehension was 12.93/20 for systematic vs 12.23/20 for self-regulated. This was not significant (p = .056 one-tailed, d = 0.24; BF ≈ 2.49 in favour of no difference).
  - Study time was about 37 min in both groups.
  - Breaks taken: about 3.1 (systematic) vs 1.8 (self-regulated).
  - Self-regulated breaks were linked to **higher energy, concentration and motivation and less distraction**. Fatigue, boredom, stress and interest did not differ.
  - Self-regulated breaks were rated as more acceptable and attractive, and were preferred.
  — [PMC13405840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/)
- Qualitative: many students in both groups wanted **longer, less frequent breaks**, and some found the 3-min breaks too short and disruptive. The authors note that "the effectiveness of systematic breaks may depend on the specific timing and duration of the break schedule." — [PMC13405840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/)
- Limitations:
  - The test's reliability was low (α = .54).
  - Most self-regulated participants took only one break.
  - The 12/3 schedule is shorter than standard Pomodoro.
  - It was a single 60-min session at one university.
  — [PMC13405840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/)

**Related evidence on micro-breaks: Albulescu et al. (2022), *PLOS ONE* 17(8):e0272460 [PR, meta-analysis, Romania]**
- Scope: 22 samples, N = 2,335. Micro-breaks were defined as breaks of 10 min or less. — [DOAJ/PLOS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f); [ScienceDaily](https://Www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/08/220831152754.htm)
- Well-being: micro-breaks increased vigor (d = 0.36, k = 9) and reduced fatigue (d = 0.35, k = 9), both p < .001. — [DOAJ/PLOS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)
- Performance:
  - The overall effect was **not significant** (d = 0.16, p = .116). It was significant only for tasks with lower cognitive demands.
  - In a meta-regression, longer breaks went with larger performance gains.
  - The authors suggest that recovering from highly depleting tasks may need **more than 10 minutes**.
  — [DOAJ/PLOS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)

**Pomodoro origin [NON-PR]:** Francesco Cirillo designed it in the late 1980s. The standard form is 25 min of work, a 5-min break, and a 15–30 min break after four cycles. No original empirical validation exists. — [Wikipedia: Pomodoro Technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomodoro_Technique); [DeskTime blog](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17)
- Wikipedia relays a 2025 scoping review in *BMC Medical Education* (Ogut, on anatomy learning) saying that time-structured Pomodoro interventions "consistently improved focus." A blog says it covered 32 studies and more than 5,000 participants. I did **not** verify either claim. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomodoro_Technique)

### Inferences
- In the best available evidence, fixed vs self-regulated breaks makes **no difference to output**. What changes is mood, fatigue and how acceptable people find the schedule. An app should not claim that Pomodoro "boosts productivity."
- The mood results conflict across studies. Fixed breaks seem to help people who otherwise *under-break*: in Biwer 2023, self-regulators ran long sessions and long breaks. Rigid breaks frustrate people who are in flow (Smits 2025; Göksu 2026). A **hybrid** design may fit both: a default timer with a soft prompt to break, the option to extend when in flow, and a hard cap on session length.
- Very short breaks every 12–25 minutes are often felt as disruptive. Students asked for longer, less frequent breaks. Albulescu's finding that longer breaks improve performance also points the same way. Both support **~50–60 min of work followed by a ~10–15 min break** over 25/5 for a 1-hour-session app. That is an inference, not a tested schedule.
- All of these RCT-like studies used **students in sessions of 1–8 hours**. None tested knowledge workers over weeks.

### Gaps
- I could not get the Smits 2025 full text (403), so its means, SDs and test statistics are unverified.
- I found no peer-reviewed RCT testing 50/10, 52/17 or 90/20 head to head against 25/5.
- I found no long-term (weeks or months) field trial of Pomodoro among adult workers.
- I could not verify Ogut's 2025 BMC Medical Education scoping review.

## Q2. Where 52/17 comes from, and how credible it is (DeskTime / Draugiem Group)

### Takeaway
52/17 is **vendor-generated, non-peer-reviewed descriptive data**. It describes the top 10% of DeskTime users by DeskTime's own "productive apps" metric. DeskTime published no sample size, statistics or causal test. DeskTime's own later reruns gave **80/17** (February 2020) and **112/26** (2021), so the ratio is not a stable optimum.

### Cited Findings
- 2014 original [NON-PR]:
  - DeskTime (Draugiem Group, Latvia) analysed the computer use of its **top 10% most productive users** over one workday.
  - "Productive" meant the highest share of time in apps and sites that DeskTime classifies as productive for each role.
  - Finding: "the most productive people work for 52 minutes, then break for 17 minutes."
  - No sample size was reported.
  — [DeskTime blog, "Does the 52-17 rule really hold up?"](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17)
- February 2020 data (pre-pandemic) [NON-PR]: 80 min of work followed by an average 17-min break. — [DeskTime blog](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17)
- 2021 rerun [NON-PR]:
  - "work for 112 minutes and then take a 26-minute break"
  - DeskTime attributes the shift to remote work, but its own February 2020 data already showed the upward trend.
  - No sample size, methods or significance tests were reported for any wave.
  — [DeskTime blog](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17); [DeskTime productivity research overview](https://desktime.com/blog/productivity-research)
- DeskTime itself concludes that there is "no one-size-fits-all approach." — [DeskTime blog](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17)
- Critique: the data is observational. It describes what high scorers did rather than identifying an optimal schedule, and the 2021 change undercuts any claim of a biological optimum. — [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/t-desktime-52-17-work-break-productivity-observational-data/); [Wikipedia: 52/17 rule](https://EN.wikipedia.com/wiki/52/17_rule)

### Inferences
- The 52/17 data has serious problems:
  - The "productivity" metric is circular. Time in "productive" apps counts as productivity, so a break lowers the score by definition. No output was measured.
  - Selecting only top performers means DeskTime cannot tell whether the rhythm causes productivity or simply comes with it.
  - It covers one workday and gives no N.
- Still, DeskTime's ~52–112-minute work blocks broadly match how real high performers work: **blocks of about an hour or longer, with breaks of 15–25 minutes**. The app's ~1-hour session sits inside that range. Present it as "a common observed rhythm," not as proven.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed replication of 52/17 or 112/26.
- DeskTime has never disclosed the N or the definitions of work and break (for example, what minimum idle time counts as a break).

## Q3. Longer breaks and recovery (lunch, 15–30 min): Trougakos, Sonnentag/Fritz, Hunter & Wu, Kühnel, Bosch

### Takeaway
The recovery literature, mostly experience-sampling and diary studies with European and US employees, finds three things:
- **Autonomy and control over the break** matter as much as the activity itself.
- **Psychological detachment** and relaxation are the main mechanisms of recovery.
- **Preferred activities, earlier breaks, and longer or more frequent breaks** recover more resources.

### Cited Findings

**Hunter & Wu (2016), "Give Me a Better Break," *Journal of Applied Psychology* 101(3):302–311, DOI 10.1037/apl0000045 [PR, USA, Baylor University]**
- Design: experience sampling with 95 employees (administrative university staff, about 83% female) over 5 workdays, yielding 959 break surveys (about 2 breaks per person per day). — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835); [Medical News Today release](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/releases/299340.php)
- Findings:
  - **Earlier breaks** (mid-morning) and **preferred activities** brought more resource recovery than late-afternoon breaks.
  - **Longer breaks and frequent short breaks** brought more resources than infrequent short breaks.
  - Recovered resources mediated effects on health symptoms and were related to emotional exhaustion, job satisfaction and organizational citizenship behaviour.
  — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835); [Medical Daily](https://www.medicaldaily.com/coffee-break-lunch-break-better-break-best-time-take-break-work-and-do-something-you-352130); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3050839/the-better-way-to-take-a-break)

**Kühnel, Zacher, de Bloom & Bledow (2017), "Take a break! Benefits of sleep and short breaks for daily work engagement," *European Journal of Work and Organizational Psychology*, DOI 10.1080/1359432X.2016.1269750 [PR, Germany/Europe]**
- Design: 107 employees, two surveys a day for 5 workdays (453 days in total).
- Findings:
  - **Self-initiated short breaks in the afternoon raised work engagement. Morning breaks did not.**
  - Breaks did **not** make up for poor sleep.
  — [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/5134); [full text PDF](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/lkcsb_research/article/6133/viewcontent/TakeABreak_EJWOP_afv.pdf)

**Trougakos, Hideg, Cheng & Beal (2014), "Lunch breaks unpacked," *Academy of Management Journal* 57(2):405–421, DOI 10.5465/amj.2011.1072 [PR, Canada/USA]**
- Design: multi-source experience sampling with administrative staff at a large university over 10 days. Coworkers rated how fatigued participants looked at the end of each day. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/10/131002125507.htm); [PolyU record](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/lunch-breaks-unpacked-the-role-of-autonomy-as-a-moderator-of-reco/)
- Findings:
  - **Lunch-break autonomy** moderates the effects of break activities.
  - For social and work activities during lunch, rising autonomy reversed the effect from *increasing* to *reducing* end-of-day fatigue.
  - Relaxation mattered most when autonomy was low.
  - The paper drew on ego-depletion theory and self-determination theory.
  — [SUFE newsletter abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361025); [HealthDay](https://consumer.healthday.com/public-health-information-30/occupational-health-news-507/working-through-lunch-may-feel-ok-680785.html)

**Bosch, Sonnentag & Pinck (2018), "What makes for a good break? A diary study on recovery experiences during lunch break," *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* 91(1):134–157, DOI 10.1111/joop.12195 [PR, Germany]**
- Control over break activities was related to higher positive affect and detachment and to lower emotional exhaustion after work.
- Relaxing activities were related to positive affect.
- Social and work-related activities showed no relationship with the outcomes.
- These findings come via a University of Vienna PDF that appears to describe this study; I did not confirm the link directly. The task brief cited "Bosch & Sonnentag 2019"; the paper I found is 2018.
— [Mannheim MADOC record](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/48355/); [Univie PDF](https://ao-psy.univie.ac.at/fileadmin/user_upload/p_ao_psy/160271.pdf)

**Sonnentag & Fritz (2007), Recovery Experience Questionnaire, *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 12(3):204–221, DOI 10.1037/1076-8998.12.3.204 [PR, Germany/USA]**
- The questionnaire defines four recovery experiences: **psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery and control**. It has 16 items in four scales of 4 items each.
- In the third study, the experiences related moderately to job stressors and well-being, and weakly to coping and personality.
— [KOPS Konstanz](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/901f4299-e3f5-46af-99cb-a27bdd4bca71); [Sonnentag measures page](https://www.sowi.uni-mannheim.de/en/sonnentag/research/measures-and-tools); [psytests REQ](https://psytests.org/work/reqen.html)

**Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah (2017), meta-analysis of detachment, *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:2072 [PR, Germany, BAuA]**
- Scope: 86 publications, k = 91 samples, N = 38,124 employees.
- Outcomes: detachment was positively related to mental and physical health, state well-being and **task performance** (small to medium effects). It was not related to physiological stress indicators or work motivation, and was slightly *negatively* related to contextual performance and creativity.
- Antecedents: job demands were negatively related to detachment (small effect).
— [PMC5233687](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5233687/); [Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full)
- Interventions to increase detachment have an average effect of d = 0.36 (separate 2021 meta-analysis). — [PubMed 34096763](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34096763/)

**Sonnentag & Kühnel (2016) [PR, Germany]**
- Work engagement was higher in the morning than in the afternoon.
- Evening detachment and morning "reattachment" predicted engagement during the day.
— [MADOC 48519](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/48519/) (as summarized in search results)

### Inferences
- For the app's long break (15–30 min, or lunch), the evidence supports making it:
  - **user-chosen**, because autonomy matters (Trougakos 2014; Bosch 2018)
  - **detaching**, meaning away from the screen and work content, because detachment is linked to well-being and task performance
  - built around **preferred activities** (Hunter & Wu)
- Suggesting "work during lunch" or forced socialising is counterproductive unless the user chooses it.
- Micro-breaks of 10 min or less restore energy but do not reliably help performance on demanding tasks. Performance gains grow with break length, which supports **one longer break every 2–3 work sessions**. That is an inference, not a tested rule.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the abstract of Trougakos, Beal, Green & Weiss (2008), "Making the break count," *AMJ* 51(1). Its details (from memory: cheerleading instructors, break activities and affective displays) are **unverified** and left out.
- I found no study that experimentally sets *how often* longer breaks should occur, for example a 15-min break every 2 hours vs every 3 hours.

## Q4. Do earlier breaks help more? How often should longer breaks occur?

### Takeaway
The evidence is **mixed**. Hunter & Wu (2016, USA) found that mid-morning breaks recover more resources than late-afternoon ones. Kühnel et al. (2017, Germany) found that **afternoon** short breaks raised engagement while morning breaks did not. The two studies measured different outcomes (resources vs engagement) and different break types. No study prescribes an optimal frequency for longer breaks.

### Cited Findings
- Earlier breaks in the shift brought more resource recovery, and longer or frequent breaks did better than infrequent short ones (N = 95, 959 breaks). — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/releases/299340.php)
- Afternoon self-initiated short breaks raised daily engagement, and morning breaks did not (N = 107, 453 days). — [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/5134)
- Engagement is higher in the morning than in the afternoon. — [MADOC 48519](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/48519/)
- Albulescu 2022: break length was positively associated with performance gains, and highly depleting tasks may need breaks longer than 10 min. — [DOAJ/PLOS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)
- Pomodoro convention [NON-PR]: a 15–30 min break after every four 25-min cycles, i.e. roughly every 2 hours. — [DeskTime blog](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomodoro_Technique)
- DeskTime [NON-PR]: high-scoring users break about every 52–112 min for 17–26 min. — [DeskTime blog](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17)

### Inferences
- One way to reconcile the two studies: do not delay the first break, which supports Hunter & Wu's "don't wait until you're exhausted," and keep breaking in the afternoon, when resources and engagement fall (Kühnel; Sonnentag & Kühnel).
- A design with a short break after every ~1-hour session and a longer 20–30-min break (or lunch) every ~2–3 sessions matches the convergent signals. **No trial has tested that exact schedule.**

### Gaps
- I found no experimental manipulation of *break timing* within the workday.
- I found no peer-reviewed dose–response evidence on the frequency of longer breaks.

## Q5. Daily capacity: how many hours of truly focused work are realistic?

### Takeaway
The often-quoted "about 4 hours a day of deep or deliberate work" comes from Ericsson et al. (1993) and its popularization by Cal Newport. It is weaker than usually presented. At the level of weekly hours, Pencavel (2014/2015) and the Iceland and UK reduced-hours trials suggest that output does not fall, and well-being improves, when working time is cut to about 32–36 hours a week.

### Cited Findings

**Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), *Psychological Review* 100:363–406 [PR, Germany, West Berlin music academy]**
- The "best" violinists had accumulated more than 10,000 hours of deliberate practice by age 20, about 2,500 more than the "good" group and about 5,000 more than the teacher group. — [Macnamara & Maitra 2019 replication, PMC6731745](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6731745/)
- The "4 hours a day" claim:
  - The paper's framing is about **1 hour a day for novices and up to about 4 hours for experienced practitioners**.
  - Critics argue the evidence behind the figure is weak (small studies, narrow tasks), and a spot-check found that the paper generalizes the limit from thin support.
  — [Ace Sounderglass epistemic spot check](https://acesounderglass.com/2019/06/21/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the-acquisition-of-expert-performance/) [NON-PR blog]; [LessWrong mirror](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/8EmPhoxgSXGSdq4Cb)
- The 2019 replication with Cleveland Institute of Music students confirmed differences in accumulated practice between skill levels. In that replication, deliberate practice explained less of the differences in performance than in the original. — [PMC6731745](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6731745/); [The Strad](https://www.thestrad.com/news/10000-hours-of-practice-doesnt-buy-you-talent-new-study-says/9364.article)
- Daily-limit figures quoted online (elite violinists practising about 4 hours a day in 60–90-min sessions) appear only in secondary [NON-PR] sources. — [Unhustle Substack](https://unhustle.substack.com/p/the-10000-hour-rule-myth-and-the)

**Newport (*Deep Work*, 2016) [NON-PR, book]**
- About 1 hour a day of deep work for beginners, expanding to about 4 hours for experienced practitioners, "rarely more." Newport traces this to deliberate-practice research and has said other kinds of deep work can run longer. — [LessWrong discussion quoting Newport](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/8EmPhoxgSXGSdq4Cb/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the)

**Pencavel (2014), "The Productivity of Working Hours," IZA DP 8129; published in *Economic Journal* 125 (2015):2052–2076 [PR, UK WWI munitions data]**
- Output is nonlinear in hours: proportional below a threshold, then rising at a decreasing rate. — [IZA abstract](https://conference.iza.org/en/webcontent/publications/papers/viewAbstract?dp_id=8129); [Stanford SIEPR](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/productivity-working-hours)
- Exact numbers, from **secondary** coverage only:
  - the threshold is about 49 hours a week
  - output differed little between 56 and 70 hours a week
  — [GovExec](https://www.govexec.com/management/2014/12/you-really-can-work-longer-hours-without-killing-your-productivity/101394); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/?p=54149)
- Caveat: these were mostly female manual munition workers in 1914–18, not knowledge workers.

**UK four-day-week pilot, June–December 2022 [grey literature; Autonomy, 4 Day Week Global, Cambridge and Boston College]**
- 61 companies and about 2,900 workers, with no pay cut and a "substantial" cut in hours. — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/21/1158507132/uk-study-companies-four-day-workweek); [Full report](https://www.4dayweek.com/uk-full-report-2023)
- Outcomes:
  - 56 of the 61 firms (92%) continued the four-day week, and 18 made it permanent.
  - 39% of employees reported less stress and 71% less burnout.
  - Staff departures fell by 57% (secondary source).
  — [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/more-than-60-companies-tried-four-day-work-week-results-show-why-92-are-keeping-it); [Poulantzas summary](https://poulantzas.gr/en/yliko/four-day-workweek-results-of-the-pilot-trial-in-the-united-kingdom/)
- Caveats: firms self-selected, there was no control group, and data were largely self-reported.

**Iceland public-sector trials (Reykjavík City 2014/15–2019; national government 2017–2021) [grey literature; Alda/Autonomy report]**
- More than 2,500 workers (over 1% of the workforce) moved from 40 to 35–36 hours a week with no pay cut. Workplaces ranged from offices and preschools to social services and hospitals.
- Productivity and service provision did not fall, and stress and burnout improved.
- About 86% of Iceland's workforce has since moved to shorter hours **or gained the right** to them.
— [Alda/Autonomy report PDF](https://en.alda.is/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/ICELAND_4DW.pdf); [Alda](https://en.alda.is/2021/07/04/going-public-icelands-journey-to-a-shorter-working-week/); [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/uk/news/breaking-news/largest-ever-trial-of-shorter-working-week-reveals-findings-260014.aspx)
- Note: the dates differ between news coverage and the report's own table of contents. — [Alda report](https://en.alda.is/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/ICELAND_4DW.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a 1-hour-session app, a sensible default ceiling is **about 4 focused sessions a day** (about 4 hours of deep work), with a range of **2–6 sessions**. Frame it as a heuristic (Ericsson/Newport), not a measured physiological limit.
- The weekly-hours evidence (Pencavel; Iceland; UK) shows diminishing returns past roughly 35–49 hours. It is consistent with "fewer, protected focused hours," but it does not measure *deep-focus* hours directly.
- Suggested feature: track daily focused sessions and nudge the user after the 4th or 5th rather than gamifying unlimited streaks.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that directly measures how many hours of cognitively demanding knowledge work per day can be sustained before output declines.
- I did not verify the exact Pencavel numbers against the Economic Journal text.
- I did not verify whether the Iceland trials achieved shorter hours partly by restructuring or cutting coffee breaks. I recall this from memory only. If true, it is directly relevant to break design and worth checking in the Alda report.

## Q6. Ego depletion and willpower: replication-crisis caveats and implications

### Takeaway
The original "limited willpower resource" model (Baumeister) **failed a large preregistered multilab replication** (Hagger et al. 2016, d = 0.04, CI including zero), though its proponents dispute the protocol. Break design should not rest on "refilling a willpower tank." Better-supported mechanisms are fatigue and mood, motivation and autonomy (self-determination theory), and detachment and recovery.

### Cited Findings
- Hagger et al. (2016), "A multilab preregistered replication of the ego-depletion effect," *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(4):546–573 [PR, 23 labs internationally]:
  - total N = 2,141, using the Sripada et al. sequential-task protocol
  - pooled **d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]**
  - by comparison, the earlier uncorrected meta-analytic estimate was d = 0.62 (Hagger et al. 2010)
  — [PMC4971805 / Hagger PDF](https://nature.berkeley.edu/garbelottoat/wp-content/uploads/hagger-chatzisarantis-2016.pdf); [Maastricht CRIS](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/a-multilab-preregistered-replication-of-the-ego-depletion-effect/)
- Rebuttal: Baumeister & Vohs called the project a "Misguided Effort With Elusive Implications," arguing that the depleting task may not have worked. Dang made similar points. — [Discover Magazine](https://discovermagazine.com/mind/the-end-of-ego-depletion-theory)
- Applied break research still uses ego-depletion framing. For example, Trougakos 2014 draws on Muraven & Baumeister (2000) *together with* self-determination theory. — [SUFE abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361025)
- In the student break studies, the outcomes that differ between conditions are **subjective fatigue, motivation and concentration**, not task completion. This fits motivational and affective accounts better than a strict resource-depletion account. — [PubMed 36859717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/); [PMC13405840](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405840/)

### Inferences
- App copy should avoid "your willpower is depleted, recharge it." Use framings such as "fatigue builds over long stretches," "a break restores energy and mood," and "detach from the task."
- Because autonomy keeps appearing as a moderator (Trougakos 2014; Bosch 2018; the acceptability results in Göksu 2026), the app should let users choose break activities and lengths within guardrails, rather than enforcing rigid lockouts.

### Gaps
- I did not cover post-2016 replications such as Vohs et al. 2021 (a multisite paradigmatic replication), which also reported a very small or null effect. I did not search for it, so it is unverified here.
